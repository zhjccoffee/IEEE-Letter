\begin{abstract}
Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has shown promise for controlling
musculoskeletal locomotion, but direct muscle-level control requires
exploration in a high-dimensional and highly redundant action space. This
letter investigates a low-dimensional reflex-informed DRL pipeline for musculoskeletal
gait generation. Instead of directly commanding all muscle
excitations, the proposed residual reflex pipeline uses an existing
reflex-based controller as a structured neuromechanical prior inspired by
spinal sensory feedback pathways, and trains a
DRL policy to modulate four selected reflex parameters through residual
actions. The policy therefore operates in a compact reflex-parameter space
while preserving the biomechanical feedback structure embedded in the
reflex controller. The pipeline is validated in a Hyfydy-based planar
musculoskeletal model using fixed-speed walking tasks at 0.8, 1.0, and
1.2~m/s. The proposed controller is compared with an end-to-end baseline
that directly controls muscle-level actions. Although the end-to-end
baseline can generate walking motions, its normalized gait-cycle profiles
show substantial deviations from reference gait, especially in knee
kinematics and vertical ground reaction force. In contrast, the residual
reflex controller achieves higher reference-envelope gait-cycle scores across the
tested speeds and produces more symmetric and repeatable gait cycles. These
results suggest that reducing the effective action space through residual
reflex modulation can improve the physiological plausibility of learned
musculoskeletal walking beyond what is indicated by task reward alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{H}{uman} walking is generated by the interaction of neural
feedback, muscle--tendon dynamics, skeletal mechanics, and contact with the
environment. Neuromusculoskeletal and musculoskeletal models provide a useful
platform for studying this interaction because they explicitly connect muscle
activation to joint motion and ground reaction forces. However, controlling
such models remains difficult. The system is highly overactuated, the muscle
dynamics are strongly coupled, and many different activation patterns can
produce similar task-level motion. As a result, a controller that achieves a
locomotion objective may still produce gait that is irregular, asymmetric, or
physiologically implausible.

Biologically inspired reflex controllers provide one way to address this
problem. Muscle reflex models encode local sensory--motor feedback pathways
inspired by spinal reflex mechanisms and have been shown to generate
human-like walking dynamics \cite{geyer2010}. Predictive simulation tools further make it possible to optimize and
evaluate such controllers in simulations of biological motion. Nevertheless, reflex-based
controllers typically depend on carefully selected gains, thresholds, and
offsets. These parameters are often tuned manually or optimized offline, and
their effects can be sensitive to the musculoskeletal model and task condition.

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) offers a complementary route by learning
closed-loop policies directly from interaction with simulation. Recent work on
musculoskeletal reinforcement learning has highlighted the difficulty of
exploration in high-dimensional muscle-level action spaces. For example,
DEP-RL addresses this issue by improving embodied exploration while still
operating in the original overactuated control space \cite{deprl2023}. This
raises a related question: rather than improving exploration in the full muscle
excitation space, can the effective action space itself be reduced by learning
at the level of structured reflex modulation?

This letter investigates that question through a low-dimensional
reflex-informed DRL pipeline
for musculoskeletal walking. Instead of replacing the reflex
controller with an end-to-end policy, the proposed method keeps the reflex
controller as a low-level neuromuscular feedback prior and trains a policy to output
residual adjustments to a small set of selected reflex parameters. The role of
DRL is therefore changed from direct muscle excitation control to modulation of
an interpretable neuromechanical controller. This idea is related in spirit to
residual reinforcement learning \cite{residualrl2019}, but is applied here to
reflex-parameter modulation in a muscle-driven gait environment.

The main contributions of this letter are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A residual reflex action representation is formulated for
    musculoskeletal gait generation. The learned policy modulates four selected
    reflex parameters instead of directly commanding all muscle excitations.
    \item The proposed pipeline is implemented in a Hyfydy-based planar     musculoskeletal environment. As an initial validation, it is compared with
    an end-to-end muscle-level DRL baseline on three fixed-speed walking tasks.
    \item The learned gaits are evaluated using normalized gait-cycle
    kinematics and vertical ground reaction force (GRF) profiles. The results
    show that residual reflex modulation improves reference gait-cycle similarity,
    especially for knee kinematics, ankle kinematics, and GRF morphology.
    \item The comparison demonstrates that high task reward or successful
    walking alone is not sufficient evidence of physiologically plausible
    musculoskeletal gait, motivating gait-quality evaluation beyond reward.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this letter is organized as follows: Section II presents the residual reflex control formulation and learning pipeline; Section III describes the controlled walking validation setup and evaluation metrics; Section IV reports and analyzes the results; and Section V concludes the paper and outlines
future work.


\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Residual-Reflex Control Architecture}
The proposed method treats the reflex controller as a structured low-level
motor prior inspired by spinal sensory feedback circuits and learns only a
compact residual modulation on top of it. As
shown in Fig.~\ref{framwork}, the musculoskeletal model returns proprioceptive
and kinematic states to a high-level DRL policy. The policy outputs a
low-dimensional residual action, which modifies selected reflex parameters
before the reflex controller generates muscle excitations. The resulting muscle
excitations are then transformed into skeletal motion and ground reaction forces
by the musculoskeletal simulator. This hierarchy changes the role of DRL from
direct muscle excitation control to reflex-parameter modulation.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{framwork.png}}
\caption{Low-dimensional reflex-informed DRL pipeline. The learned policy does
not directly command all muscle excitations. Instead, it modulates selected
parameters of a neural-feedback-inspired reflex controller, which then produces
muscle-level stimulation for the
musculoskeletal model.}
\label{framwork}
\end{figure}

Let $\mathbf{s}_t$ denote the state observed by the policy at time $t$. The
state contains muscle-level signals, foot states, whole-body kinematics, and
joint-space variables:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{s}_t = [
&\mathbf{l}_t,\dot{\mathbf{l}}_t,\mathbf{f}_t,\mathbf{e}_t,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t,\mathbf{p}_{feet,t},\\
&\mathbf{q}_t,\dot{\mathbf{q}}_t,\theta_t,\dot{\theta}_t],
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{l}$, $\dot{\mathbf{l}}$, and $\mathbf{f}$ are muscle fiber
lengths, velocities, and forces; $\mathbf{e}$ and $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ are
muscle excitations and activations; $\mathbf{p}_{feet}$ represents foot
positions; $\mathbf{q}$ and $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ are joint angles and angular
velocities; and $\theta$ is the trunk pitch angle.

\subsection{Reflex Controller}
The low-level controller is a phase-dependent reflex controller inspired by
neuromuscular feedback pathways, including muscle-spindle-like length and
velocity feedback and Golgi-tendon-organ-like force feedback. The gait cycle is divided into stance,
liftoff, swing, and landing-related subphases. In each subphase, sparse
reflex pathways map sensory feedback to target muscle stimulation. For a target
muscle $i$ and a source muscle $j$, the controller includes length, velocity,
and force feedback terms:
\begin{align}
u_{L,i\leftarrow j} &= k_{L,i\leftarrow j}
\max(0,\tilde{l}_j(t-t_D)-\tilde{l}_{0,i\leftarrow j}),\\
u_{V,i\leftarrow j} &= k_{V,i\leftarrow j}
\max(0,\tilde{v}_j(t-t_D)-\tilde{v}_{0,i\leftarrow j}),\\
u_{F,i\leftarrow j} &= k_{F,i\leftarrow j}
(\tilde{f}_j(t-t_D)-\tilde{f}_{0,i\leftarrow j}),
\end{align}
where $\tilde{l}$, $\tilde{v}$, and $\tilde{f}$ are normalized muscle length,
velocity, and force; $k_L$, $k_V$, and $k_F$ are reflex gains; and $t_D$ is a
sensorimotor delay. A reflex-like postural feedback term is also used to
couple trunk pitch errors to selected lower-limb muscles:
\begin{equation}
u_{\text{PD},i}=k_{P,i}(\theta(t-t_D)-\theta_{0,i})+
k_{D,i}\dot{\theta}(t-t_D).
\end{equation}
The total stimulation of muscle $i$ is the sum of feedforward, local feedback,
and postural feedback components:
\begin{equation}
u_i = u_{C,i}+u_{\text{PD},i}
+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}(u_{L,i\leftarrow j}
+u_{V,i\leftarrow j}+u_{F,i\leftarrow j}).
\end{equation}
This reflex structure provides immediate sensory feedback and a biomechanical
inductive bias before policy learning is applied.

\subsection{Residual Action Space}
Instead of allowing the policy to directly command all muscle excitations, the
proposed controller uses a four-dimensional residual action
$\mathbf{a}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{4}$. Each action component modulates one selected
reflex parameter around its nominal value:
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)=p_{i,0}(1+\lambda a_i(t)),
\end{equation}
where $p_{i,0}$ is the nominal reflex parameter and $\lambda$ is the action
scale. In the present implementation, $\lambda=0.5$. Thus $a_i(t)=0$ recovers
the underlying reflex controller, while nonzero actions produce state-dependent
residual modulation.

The pathway labels in Table~\ref{tab:residual_actions} follow the controller
implementation notation: the binary code after ``S'' denotes the gait subphases
in which the corresponding reflex pathway is active, and the suffix specifies
the muscle and reflex parameter being modulated.

These four parameters were selected because they act on gait-critical pathways:
hip flexion/extension for leg swing and placement, knee extension for stance
support, and ankle plantarflexion for support and propulsion. The purpose of
this selection is not to claim global optimality of the four parameters, but to
test whether a small and interpretable reflex-parameter action space can guide
DRL toward more natural gait than direct muscle-level control.

\subsection{Policy Learning}
The policy is trained with Maximum a Posteriori Policy Optimization (MPO), an
off-policy actor--critic algorithm for continuous control. The same optimizer
is used for both the proposed residual-reflex controller and the end-to-end
baseline. Therefore, the main experimental comparison isolates the control
representation: low-dimensional reflex-parameter modulation versus direct
muscle-level action.

At each time step, the policy samples an action from a Gaussian action
distribution conditioned on $\mathbf{s}_t$. The action is applied to the
controller, the simulator advances one step, and the transition
$(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t,r_t,\mathbf{s}_{t+1})$ is stored for off-policy
updates. The base reward combines target-speed tracking ($r_{vel}$) with
regularization terms for excessive ground reaction force ($r_{grf}$), abrupt
excitation changes ($r_{smooth}$), muscle usage ($r_{musc}$), joint-limit
torques ($r_{constr}$), and non-foot self-contact ($r_{self}$):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r_t^{base} =\,
&w_{vel}r_{vel}+w_{grf}r_{grf}+w_{smooth}r_{smooth}\\
&+w_{musc}r_{musc}+w_{constr}r_{constr}+w_{self}r_{self}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For the residual-reflex controller, additional terms regularize pelvis pitch
($r_{\theta}$), pelvis pitch rate ($r_{\dot{\theta}}$), vertical
center-of-mass velocity ($r_{com}$), and residual-action changes
($r_{\Delta a}$):
\begin{equation}
r_t = r_t^{base} + w_{\theta}r_{\theta}
+w_{\dot{\theta}}r_{\dot{\theta}}+w_{com}r_{com}+w_{\Delta a}r_{\Delta a}.
\end{equation}
Some terms may have zero weight in a given experiment; the fixed-speed
experiments reported here used the same base reward structure for the
residual-reflex and end-to-end policies, with the additional posture and
residual-action regularization applied to the residual-reflex controller.
The reward is used for policy learning only. Final gait quality is assessed
using gait-cycle kinematics, GRF morphology, left--right symmetry, and
cycle-to-cycle repeatability rather than episodic reward alone.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Environments and Tasks}\label{sec:experiments}
\subsubsection{Simulation Environment}
The experiments were conducted in a two-dimensional planar musculoskeletal
model implemented with the Hyfydy physics engine \cite{hyfydy}. The model contains the pelvis, trunk, and
bilateral lower-limb segments, with sagittal-plane hip, knee, and ankle motion.
Actuation is provided by 18 Hill-type muscles, with nine muscles per leg. Foot--ground interaction is modeled through discrete contact elements, and the
resulting vertical ground reaction forces are recorded for gait analysis.

\subsubsection{Compared Control Interfaces}
Two DRL policy interfaces were compared. The proposed \emph{Residual Reflex}
controller uses the four-dimensional reflex-parameter action space described in
Table~\ref{tab:residual_actions}. The baseline controller is an
\emph{End-to-End} muscle-control policy without the reflex modulation
structure. It directly outputs muscle-level control actions for the same
musculoskeletal model. Both controllers were trained with MPO-based off-policy
reinforcement learning. The current experiments do not use DEP; DEP-RL is
discussed as related work because it motivates the difficulty of
high-dimensional musculoskeletal action spaces.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
Policies were evaluated at three fixed target speeds: 0.8, 1.0, and
1.2~m/s. These fixed-speed tasks are used as controlled validation cases for
the residual reflex pipeline, rather than as the intended limit of the method.
For each trained policy, a representative walking trajectory was exported as
an STO file and analyzed using the reference-envelope gait-cycle analysis
procedure.
The gait cycle was normalized to 0--100\%, and the following profiles were
computed for both legs: pelvic tilt, hip angle, knee angle, ankle angle, and
vertical GRF. The reference envelopes were obtained from a gait-analysis
template containing normative lower and upper bounds for each normalized
gait-cycle profile. For each quantity, all detected left and right gait cycles
were first time-normalized and averaged to obtain a mean simulated profile
$\bar{y}(x)$, where $x$ denotes gait-cycle percentage. Let
$y_{\min}(x_k)$ and $y_{\max}(x_k)$ denote the lower and upper reference
envelope values at the template sample points $x_k$. The normalized envelope
violation is computed as
\begin{equation}
e = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
\frac{
\left[y_{\min}(x_k)-\bar{y}(x_k)\right]_+
+ \left[\bar{y}(x_k)-y_{\max}(x_k)\right]_+
}{
\max\left(0.01, y_{\max}(x_k)-y_{\min}(x_k)\right)
}.
\end{equation}
where $[z]_+=\max(z,0)$.
The corresponding gait-cycle score is then defined as
\begin{equation}
S = 100 \cdot \mathrm{clip}(1-e,0,1).
\end{equation}
Thus, a score of 100 indicates that the averaged trajectory stays entirely
within the reference envelope, whereas lower scores indicate larger normalized
deviations from the reference gait pattern. The overall score is the average
of the component scores for pelvic tilt, hip angle, knee angle, ankle angle,
and vertical GRF.

In addition to the gait-cycle component scores, we computed two descriptive gait
quality measures from the detected gait cycles. First, left--right waveform
symmetry was quantified as the root-mean-square difference between the average
left and right normalized profiles. Second, stride-to-stride repeatability was
quantified using the coefficient of variation (CV) of stride time and stride
length. These measures are used to describe gait regularity and bilateral
symmetry rather than fall robustness.

We also report spatiotemporal gait measures, including realized walking speed,
absolute speed tracking error, stride length, and stride time. These metrics
indicate whether the generated trajectories satisfy the commanded locomotion
task, while the reference-envelope and symmetry metrics assess the quality of
the resulting gait pattern.

task-level metrics complement the gait-cycle shape analysis: a controller may

robustness, since no external perturbation experiments are included in the

\subsection{Discussion}
The results indicate that the main benefit of residual reflex learning is not
necessarily a higher episodic reward, but a more structured and physiologically
plausible gait pattern. In the current data, end-to-end policies can achieve
successful walking and high task-level returns, yet their gait-cycle profiles
may deviate substantially from reference kinematics and GRF. This observation
suggests that reward optimization alone is insufficient for evaluating learned
musculoskeletal gait. A controller should also be assessed through gait-cycle
kinematics, GRF morphology, bilateral symmetry, and cycle-to-cycle
repeatability.

The current study has several limitations. First, all reported experiments use
a single training seed for each configuration, so the results should be
interpreted as trajectory-level evidence rather than training-seed statistics.
Second, the current validation focuses on fixed-speed walking and does not yet
include online speed transitions, external perturbation recovery, or true
metabolic cost estimation. Third, the model is planar, and extension to
three-dimensional locomotion remains future work. These limitations define the
next experimental steps, but they do not affect the central finding that a
low-dimensional residual reflex action space can improve gait plausibility
relative to direct muscle-level control within the present validation setting.


\section{Conclusion}
This letter presented a residual reflex DRL pipeline for musculoskeletal gait
generation. Rather than directly commanding all muscle
excitations, the learned policy modulates four selected reflex parameters
around a reflex-based controller. This formulation reduces the effective action
space and preserves a structured neuromechanical prior within the control loop.

As an initial validation, experiments in a Hyfydy-based planar musculoskeletal
model showed that the residual reflex controller produced gait cycles with
higher reference-envelope gait-cycle scores than an end-to-end muscle-control
baseline at 0.8, 1.0, and 1.2~m/s.
The improvement was most evident in knee kinematics, ankle kinematics, and
vertical GRF morphology. Additional cycle-level analysis showed lower
left--right waveform differences and lower stride variability, indicating more
regular and symmetric gait patterns.

The results support the view that task reward or successful walking alone is
not sufficient for evaluating learned musculoskeletal locomotion. Future work
will include multi-seed training statistics, controlled perturbation-recovery
tests, and effort or metabolic-cost analysis using validated biomechanical
energy models.
