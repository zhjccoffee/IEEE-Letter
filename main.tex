\begin{abstract}
Musculoskeletal gait generation is important for studying human locomotion,
developing assistive systems, and building bio-inspired robots. These
applications require controllers that can coordinate many redundant muscles
while preserving natural joint motion, realistic contact loading, and
adaptability to changes in the body or environment. However,
learning muscle-driven gait controllers remains challenging because direct
muscle-level deep reinforcement learning (DRL) policies typically require
exploration in a
high-dimensional and redundant action space, while traditional
optimization-based approaches often produce fixed controller parameters that do
not readily adapt to changes in the body or environment. This study proposes
Residual-Reflex RL, a low-dimensional and interpretable reflex-informed DRL
framework that treats a phase-dependent reflex controller as a neuromechanical
prior and learns residual modulation of selected reflex pathways. Instead of
directly commanding all muscles, the policy adjusts four gait-relevant reflex
parameters associated with hip swing, ankle propulsion, and stance support.
Experiments with a musculoskeletal model show that the proposed controller produces
human-like, stable, and repeatable gait, while adapting to plantarflexor weakness
and external push perturbations without retraining. These findings demonstrate
that the Residual-Reflex-Informed DRL framework can place learning at an
interpretable reflex-parameter level, enabling adaptive muscle-driven gait
generation without abandoning neuromuscular feedback structure.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Deep reinforcement learning, gait generation, musculoskeletal
simulation, reflex control, residual reinforcement learning}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Muscle-driven characters promise more than visually plausible animation. By
connecting neural stimulation, muscle force, skeletal motion, and physical
contact, they provide a computational account of how locomotion emerges from the
body. Such characters serve as computational bodies for studying human movement
and as testbeds for bio-inspired assistive and robotic systems
\cite{anderson2001,geijtenbeek2013,lee2019,degroote2021,uchida2020}. This promise
remains incomplete, however, when a controller reproduces only one nominal gait
for an intact body in an undisturbed environment. Humans routinely preserve
recognizable coordination when their physical capacity or surroundings change.
Reduced plantarflexor capacity can be accommodated through a slower,
redistributed gait, while an unexpected push evokes corrective steps without
requiring locomotion to be learned again. A character that walks only under
nominal conditions captures a motion, but not the adaptive organization that
makes human locomotion compelling.

Human locomotion provides a useful clue for how such adaptability can be
organized. Walking is not acquired as an arbitrary mapping from body state to
every individual muscle excitation. The neuromuscular system provides spinal
sensorimotor pathways that rapidly transform muscle and body feedback into
corrective responses \cite{grillner2020}. Locomotor primitives present early in
life are retained and expanded during development \cite{dominici2011}, while
continued interaction with the environment refines sensorimotor coordination
\cite{wolpert2011}. This layered organization allows coordination to improve
without discarding the feedback mechanisms that stabilize the body. When
physical capacity or external conditions change, adaptation can therefore
reshape an existing sensorimotor organization rather than reconstructing muscle
control from scratch.

Translating this layered organization into a learned muscle-driven controller is
nevertheless difficult. At the muscle level, locomotion is highly redundant.
Many excitation patterns can realize the same task while producing different
joint coordination and contact loading. Asking a policy to command every muscle
independently therefore requires it to rediscover useful coordination within a
large space of equivalent actions. Simply compressing that space into a fixed
coordination pattern may ease the search, but can restrict the capacity to
reorganize gait when the body or environment changes. The core challenge is
therefore to reduce redundancy in muscle-driven control while preserving
natural gait and retaining the ability to adapt coordination to changes in
physical capacity and external conditions.

These observations suggest that compactness and adaptability need not be
competing objectives. Our key insight is that adaptability can arise not from
learning more muscle commands, but from learning how to regulate a compact set
of feedback pathways that already organize muscle coordination. This shifts the
role of learning from muscle-excitation synthesis to state-dependent regulation
of neuromuscular feedback.

Based on this insight, we propose Residual-Reflex RL, a low-dimensional
residual-reflex-informed deep reinforcement learning framework for muscle-driven
gait generation. The framework retains a phase-dependent reflex controller as
the low-level feedback organization. At each control step, a learned policy
observes the musculoskeletal state and outputs residual changes to four selected
reflex gains and thresholds associated with hip swing, ankle propulsion, and
stance support. These actions reshape how sensory feedback is converted into
muscle stimulation rather than adding muscle excitations directly. The learned
interface is consequently compact and interpretable, while its state-dependent
modulation allows the underlying coordination to change continuously with the
body and environment. We evaluate nominal gait against direct muscle-control
policies, and test the same trained controller under plantarflexor weakness and
external push perturbations. The resulting gait is natural and repeatable under
nominal conditions, while changes in muscle capacity and external disturbances
are accommodated without retraining.

This study makes three main contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We formulate muscle-driven gait generation as state-dependent residual
    modulation of a phase-dependent reflex controller, allowing learning to
    reshape neuromuscular feedback without replacing it with direct
    muscle-excitation synthesis.
    \item We develop a four-dimensional residual-reflex action space grounded in
    gait-critical hip, knee, and ankle feedback pathways, providing an
    interpretable interface between learned actions and neuromechanical function.
    \item We evaluate the framework in nominal walking, plantarflexor weakness,
    and external push perturbation tests, comparing against direct muscle-action
    DRL baselines and a CMA-ES-optimized reflex baseline to examine gait
    plausibility, repeatability, and the ability of the trained policy to
    accommodate altered body and environment conditions without retraining.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper reviews related work, presents the residual-reflex
formulation, and evaluates the learned controller in nominal walking,
plantarflexor weakness, and external push perturbations.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Muscle-Driven Character Simulation}
Physics-based muscle-driven simulation connects control decisions to internal
muscle loading and mechanically consistent character motion. Predictive
simulation and optimal-control studies have used this representation to
generate normal and pathological gait, investigate energetic objectives, and
study how morphology or task constraints shape locomotion
\cite{anderson2001,ackermann2010,wang2010,wang2012,song2018,degroote2021}.
Trajectory optimization and direct collocation provide efficient numerical
tools for solving the resulting motion problem
\cite{falisse2019,dembia2020}, while character-animation systems have improved
the scalability and visual quality of muscle-actuated control
\cite{geijtenbeek2013,lee2019}. These methods establish that realistic motion
can emerge from muscle and contact dynamics. However, optimizing a trajectory
or a set of controller parameters offline does not by itself provide a policy
that changes its coordination strategy online when the body model or external
loading changes.

\subsection{Structured Neuromuscular Control}
Biologically motivated controllers reduce muscle redundancy by organizing
control through central pattern generators, muscle synergies, and sensory
feedback pathways \cite{ijspeert2008,davella2003,meyer2016,dzeladini2014}.
Reflex-based controllers are particularly relevant to muscle-driven gait
because muscle length, velocity, and force feedback can be mapped directly to
stimulation \cite{geyer2010,song2015}. Their feedback structure has produced
stable walking and has supported studies of speed regulation, perturbation
responses, and energy-efficient locomotion
\cite{batts2015,koseki2024,schumacher2025}. The same compact structure also
makes individual gains and thresholds biomechanically interpretable. In most
implementations, however, these parameters are hand selected or optimized by an
offline search and then held fixed during evaluation. Our work retains the
reflex pathways but turns selected parameters into a state-dependent learning
interface.

\subsection{Learning-Based Muscle Control}
Deep reinforcement learning has produced complex locomotion for physics-based
characters and muscle-driven models
\cite{peng2017,peng2018,weng2021,devree2021,ogum2024}. End-to-end approaches
learn muscle activations or excitations directly, avoiding a prescribed
trajectory but exposing the policy to a redundant muscle-level action space.
Hierarchical representations, imitation objectives, and structured exploration
have been introduced to improve learning and motion quality
\cite{song2021,nowakowski2021,su2023}. DEP-RL, for example, introduces
embodied exploratory feedback that is effective in overactuated systems, while
retaining a direct muscle-action interface \cite{deprl2023}. Other hybrid
approaches combine learning with CPG or reflex structure
\cite{li2024,schumacher2025}. Residual-Reflex RL differs in where the learned
action is applied. It neither adds a correction to a reference joint trajectory
nor outputs a complete muscle-excitation vector. Instead, it continuously
modulates a small set of reflex gains and thresholds whose downstream effects
remain mediated by sensory feedback and muscle mechanics.


\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Framework Overview}
Figure~\ref{framwork} summarizes the framework as three coupled modules. In
module (a), the rollout policy $\pi(\mathbf{s};\theta_{\mathrm{now}})$ observes the current
musculoskeletal state $\mathbf{s}_t$ and produces the four-dimensional residual
action $\mathbf{a}_t$. In module (b), this action changes four parameters of the
phase-dependent reflex controller described below. The modulated controller
maps delayed muscle and body feedback to muscle stimulation; muscle activation
and muscle--tendon forces then advance the skeletal dynamics and generate the
next state and reward. Module (c) stores the collected transitions in a replay
buffer $\mathcal{D}$, trains a critic $q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a};\omega)$, and
updates the policy to $\pi(\mathbf{s};\theta_{\mathrm{new}})$ using off-policy
Maximum a Posteriori Policy Optimization (MPO) \cite{mpo2018}.

During execution, modules (a) and (b) form a state-feedback loop from
$\mathbf{s}_t$ through residual parameter modulation and muscle stimulation to
$\mathbf{s}_{t+1}$. During learning, module (c) forms a second loop that reuses
the collected transitions to improve the rollout policy. The following sections
define the musculoskeletal dynamics, the phase-dependent reflex pathways, the
four-dimensional residual parameterization, and the MPO update used in these
loops.

\subsection{Musculoskeletal Model}
\label{sec:musculoskeletal-model}
\paragraph{Simulation platform and character.}
The physiological environment in module (b) is implemented with the Hyfydy
musculoskeletal simulation engine \cite{hyfydy} through a Python-based
SCONE Gym interface. We use the sagittal-plane H0918v2j human model, which
contains a pelvis, a rigidly attached torso, and bilateral thigh, shank, and
foot segments. Its nine generalized coordinates comprise horizontal and
vertical pelvis translation, pelvis tilt, and bilateral hip flexion, knee
flexion, and ankle plantarflexion--dorsiflexion. The character is actuated by
18 Hill-type muscle--tendon units, corresponding to nine muscle groups on each
leg: tibialis anterior (TA), soleus (SOL), gastrocnemius (GAS), vasti (VAS),
biceps femoris short head (BIF), rectus femoris (FEM), iliopsoas (ILI),
hamstrings (HAM), and gluteus maximus (GLU). Each foot interacts with the
rigid ground through spherical heel and toe contact elements. The controller
is updated at fixed 0.005s intervals. Panels (b1) and (b2) of
Fig.~\ref{framwork} show the simulated character and the modeled muscle groups,
respectively.

As illustrated by the muscle paths in panel (b2) of Fig.~\ref{framwork}, GLU
and HAM contribute primarily to hip extension,
whereas ILI and FEM contribute to hip flexion and leg advancement. VAS and BIF
provide knee extension and flexion, respectively. At the ankle, SOL and GAS
generate plantarflexion for stance support and propulsion, while TA produces
dorsiflexion for foot clearance and landing. The biarticular HAM, FEM, and GAS
span adjacent joints and couple their motion, contributing to the redundancy
of the muscle-driven system.

\paragraph{Muscle-driven dynamics.}
Module (b) couples the reflex controller to this character through the full
stimulation-to-motion chain. For muscle $i$, neural stimulation $u_i$ is first
filtered by activation dynamics to obtain activation $\alpha_i$. The
muscle--tendon model then converts activation, fiber length, and fiber velocity
into tensile force,
\begin{equation}
\dot{\alpha}_i=\mathcal{A}_i(\alpha_i,u_i), \qquad
F_i^{\mathrm{MT}}=\mathcal{F}_i(\alpha_i,l_i,\dot l_i),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{A}_i$ denotes the activation dynamics and $\mathcal{F}_i$
denotes the Hill-type force relation. Through configuration-dependent moment
arms, the muscle forces generate generalized joint forces,
\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{mus},k}=
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{M}_k}r_{i,k}(\mathbf{q})F_i^{\mathrm{MT}},
\end{equation}
where $r_{i,k}(\mathbf{q})$ is the signed moment arm of muscle $i$ about
generalized coordinate $k$, and $\mathcal{M}_k$ is the set of muscles spanning
that coordinate. The resulting character motion follows
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{q})\ddot{\mathbf{q}}+
\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{q},\dot{\mathbf{q}})=
\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\mathrm{mus}}+
\mathbf{J}_{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{f}_{c},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{q}$ contains the generalized coordinates,
$\mathbf{M}$ is the mass matrix, $\mathbf{h}$ collects gravitational and
velocity-dependent terms, and $\mathbf{J}_{c}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{f}_{c}$ is
the contribution of foot--ground contact. This chain makes the role of the
controller explicit: it does not prescribe joint angles, but produces muscle
stimulation whose mechanical consequences determine the motion.

\subsection{Phase-Dependent Reflex Controller}
\paragraph{Physiological feedback abstraction.}
The stimulation is generated by a phase-dependent reflex controller inspired
by spinal sensorimotor feedback \cite{grillner2020}. In the biological system,
muscle spindles encode muscle length and its rate of change, whereas Golgi tendon
organs respond to muscle tension. Afferent signals from these proprioceptors are
transformed through spinal circuitry into motoneuron drive, providing rapid
feedback before slower voluntary correction is available. The controller used
here is a computational abstraction of this organization rather than a detailed
neural model. Muscle-spindle-like pathways supply normalized length and velocity
signals, Golgi-tendon-organ-like pathways supply normalized force signals, and
their weighted contributions form the stimulation sent to the muscle activation
dynamics described above.

\paragraph{Phase-dependent topology.}
Reflex engagement also changes over the gait cycle. A finite-state controller
divides walking into EarlyStance, LateStance, Liftoff, Swing, and Landing, and
selects a sparse feedback subnetwork according to the current phase. The
implementation contains three parameter-sharing groups: an all-phase group
$\Phi_{\mathrm{all}}=\{\mathrm{ES,LS,LF,SW,LA}\}$, a stance group
$\Phi_{\mathrm{st}}=\{\mathrm{ES,LS}\}$, and a swing-related group
$\Phi_{\mathrm{sw}}=\{\mathrm{LF,SW,LA}\}$. Parameters are shared by the
phases within a group but are independent between groups. For example, the BIF
force-feedback gain used in EarlyStance and LateStance is distinct from the BIF
force-feedback gain used in Liftoff, Swing, and Landing. Consequently, the same
type of sensory feedback can serve loading in stance, propulsion near liftoff,
or limb placement during swing and landing without requiring one gain to serve
all phases. Panel (b3) of Fig.~\ref{framwork} summarizes this topology across
the nine muscle groups defined in
Sec.~\ref{sec:musculoskeletal-model}, including the ankle-related TA, SOL, and
GAS, the knee-related VAS and BIF, and the hip-related FEM, ILI, HAM, and GLU.

Let $\phi(t)$ denote the current gait phase and let $\Gamma(\phi)$ denote its
set of active parameter-sharing groups. For EarlyStance and LateStance,
$\Gamma(\phi)=\{\mathrm{all},\mathrm{st}\}$; for Liftoff, Swing, and Landing,
$\Gamma(\phi)=\{\mathrm{all},\mathrm{sw}\}$. Thus, the all-phase pathways are
always active and are combined with either the stance or swing-related
pathways. Each group $g\in\Gamma(\phi)$ forms a sparse directed feedback graph
$\mathcal{G}_{g}$. A connection
$i\leftarrow j$ uses a signal measured
at source muscle $j$ to modify stimulation of target muscle $i$. Connections
with $i=j$ implement self-feedback, whereas connections with $i\neq j$ encode
inter-muscle coordination. Their gains and thresholds are indexed by the active
phase group; an inactive connection has zero gain in the corresponding phase.
The sign and magnitude of a gain determine how strongly its sensory signal
facilitates or suppresses the target stimulation.

\paragraph{Reflex control laws.}
For an active group $g\in\Gamma(\phi(t))$, each connection contains length,
velocity, and force feedback contributions.
\begin{align}
u_{L,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}(t) &= K_{L,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}
\max(0,\tilde{l}_j(t-t_D)-\tilde{l}_{0,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}),\\
u_{V,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}(t) &= K_{V,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}
\max(0,\tilde{v}_j(t-t_D)-\tilde{v}_{0,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}),\\
u_{F,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}(t) &= K_{F,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}
(\tilde{f}_j(t-t_D)-\tilde{f}_{0,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}),
\end{align}
Here $u_{L,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}$, $u_{V,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}$, and
$u_{F,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}$ are the stimulation contributions from length,
velocity, and force feedback, respectively. The variables $\tilde{l}_j$,
$\tilde{v}_j$, and $\tilde{f}_j$ are normalized signals measured from source
muscle $j$; their instantaneous values are not phase indexed. The offsets
$\tilde{l}_{0,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}$,
$\tilde{v}_{0,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}$, and
$\tilde{f}_{0,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}$ define group-dependent feedback
thresholds, while $K_{L,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}$,
$K_{V,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}$, and $K_{F,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}$ are the
corresponding group-dependent reflex gains. The term $t_D$ is a sensorimotor
delay. A reflex-like postural feedback term is also used to couple trunk pitch
errors to selected lower-limb muscles.
\begin{equation}
u_{\text{PD},i}^{(g)}(t)=
K_{P,i}^{(g)}(\theta(t-t_D)-\theta_{0,i}^{(g)})+
K_{D,i}^{(g)}\dot{\theta}(t-t_D).
\end{equation}
Here $\theta$ and $\dot{\theta}$ are the delayed trunk pitch angle and angular
velocity, $\theta_{0,i}^{(g)}$ is the target pitch offset for muscle $i$, and
$K_{P,i}^{(g)}$ and $K_{D,i}^{(g)}$ are the proportional and derivative
postural-feedback gains for the active group.
The total stimulation of muscle $i$ is the sum of feedforward, local feedback,
and postural feedback components from all groups enabled in the current phase.
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
u_i(t) = \sum_{g\in\Gamma(\phi(t))}\Bigg[
&u_{C,i}^{(g)}+u_{\text{PD},i}^{(g)}(t)\\
&+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}_{g}}\left(u_{L,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}(t)
+u_{V,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}(t)+u_{F,i\leftarrow j}^{(g)}(t)\right)\Bigg].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $u_{C,i}^{(g)}$ is the group-dependent feedforward stimulation and
$\mathcal{M}_{g}$ is the set of source muscles connected to target $i$ in the
corresponding active group. This sparse source--target organization is important because the
reflex controller is not a collection of independent muscle servos. It couples
muscles that jointly control limb support, propulsion, swing, and posture.
The five phase gates select which terms contribute to each muscle at a given
time. Consequently, changes in muscle length, shortening velocity, force, or
trunk posture immediately alter stimulation even when the reflex parameters are
held fixed. The reflex controller therefore supplies an existing closed-loop
coordination structure on which residual learning can act.

\subsection{Four-Dimensional Residual Modulation}
The learned policy does not output the 18 muscle stimulations. Instead, it uses
a four-dimensional residual action
$\mathbf{a}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{4}$. Each action component modulates one selected
reflex parameter around its nominal value.
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)=p_{i,0}(1+\lambda a_i(t)),
\label{eq:residual_scaling}
\end{equation}
where $p_{i,0}$ is the nominal reflex parameter and $\lambda$ is the action
scale. In the present implementation, $\lambda=0.5$. Thus $a_i(t)=0$ recovers
the underlying reflex controller, while nonzero actions produce state-dependent
residual modulation.

The residual formulation preserves a meaningful zero action. When
$\mathbf{a}_t=\mathbf{0}$, the nominal reflex controller is recovered exactly;
nonzero actions reshape its feedback gains or thresholds in response to the
current state. Because the residual is applied to a controller parameter rather
than directly added to muscle stimulation, its downstream effect remains
mediated by gait phase, sensory feedback, activation dynamics, and muscle
mechanics.

The pathway labels in Table~\ref{tab:residual_actions} follow the controller
implementation notation. The five-bit code after ``S'' is the implementation's
phase-activation mask, while the mathematical superscripts state its semantic
phase group explicitly. Thus, $a_1$ modulates the swing-related hamstrings
length gain, $a_2$ changes the stance-phase iliopsoas length threshold, $a_3$
modulates the all-phase soleus force-feedback gain for ankle plantarflexion,
support, and propulsion, and $a_4$ modulates the stance-phase vasti
force-feedback gain for knee extension and stance support. The channel $a_3$
is therefore referred to as the soleus-related channel in the weakness
analysis.

These four parameters were selected because they act on gait-critical pathways,
including hip flexion/extension for leg swing and placement, knee extension for
stance support, and ankle plantarflexion for support and propulsion. This four-dimensional
residual action space concentrates policy exploration on biomechanically
meaningful feedback pathways while retaining an interpretable link between
learned actions and neuromuscular function.

\subsection{Policy Learning with MPO}
Module (c) implements the off-policy actor--critic learning loop shown in
Fig.~\ref{framwork}(c). Let $\mathbf{s}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{121}$ denote the
observation received by the policy at time $t$,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{s}_t = [
&\mathbf{l}_t,\dot{\mathbf{l}}_t,\mathbf{f}_t,\mathbf{e}_t,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t,\mathbf{p}_{feet,t},
\boldsymbol{\psi}_t,\boldsymbol{\omega}_t,\\
&\mathbf{q}_t,\dot{\mathbf{q}}_t].
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here $\mathbf{l}$, $\dot{\mathbf{l}}$, and $\mathbf{f}$ are muscle-fiber
lengths, velocities, and forces; $\mathbf{e}$ and $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ are
muscle excitations and activations; $\mathbf{p}_{feet}$ represents foot
positions relative to the body; $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}$
are body orientation and angular velocity; and $\mathbf{q}$ and
$\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ are generalized coordinates and velocities.

The actor and critic are multilayer perceptrons with two
hidden layers of 256 units and ReLU activations. The actor receives the
121-dimensional observation $\mathbf{s}_t$ and parameterizes the Gaussian rollout
policy $\pi(\mathbf{s};\theta_{\mathrm{now}})$ in module (a). For
Residual-Reflex RL, its output is the mean and standard deviation of a
four-dimensional residual-action distribution,
$\mathbf{a}_t\in[-1,1]^4$, corresponding to the reflex parameters in
Table~\ref{tab:residual_actions}. The critic receives the observation--action
pair and outputs the scalar state--action value
$q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a};\omega)$, where $\omega$ denotes critic parameters.

Transitions collected during rollout,
$(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t,r_t,\mathbf{s}_{t+1})$, are stored in the replay
buffer $\mathcal{D}$. MPO trains the critic from replayed transitions and updates
the actor from the rollout policy $\pi(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{s};
\theta_{\mathrm{now}})$ to a new policy. Its policy update separates action
improvement from parametric policy fitting. In the E-step, critic values
reweight actions sampled from the rollout policy to form a non-parametric
improved distribution
\begin{equation}
\rho(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{s})=
\frac{\pi(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{s};\theta_{\mathrm{now}})
\exp\!\left(q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a};\omega)/\eta\right)}
{Z(\mathbf{s})},
\end{equation}
where $Z(\mathbf{s})$ normalizes the distribution and the temperature $\eta$ is
chosen under an E-step KL budget. In the M-step, the actor is fitted to this
improved distribution while constraining its departure from the rollout policy,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\theta_{\mathrm{new}}=\arg\max_{\theta}\;&
\mathbb{E}_{\substack{\mathbf{s}\sim\mathcal{D}\\
\mathbf{a}\sim\rho(\cdot\mid\mathbf{s})}}
[\log \pi(\mathbf{a}\mid\mathbf{s};\theta)]\\
\mathrm{s.t.}\;&
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}\sim\mathcal{D}}
\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(
\pi(\cdot\mid\mathbf{s};\theta_{\mathrm{now}})\,\|\,
\pi(\cdot\mid\mathbf{s};\theta)
\right)\right]\le\epsilon_{\pi}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The critic supplies the value ranking used in the E-step, whereas the KL
constraint prevents the actor from changing abruptly when it fits the improved
action distribution. During training, actions are sampled from the stochastic
rollout policy for exploration; during deterministic evaluation, its mean is
used. The baseline controllers used for comparison are defined in the
experimental setting.

The reward $r_t$ stored in each transition defines the learning objective used
by the actor--critic update. The base reward combines target-speed tracking
($r_{vel}$) with
regularization terms for excessive ground reaction force ($r_{grf}$), abrupt
excitation changes ($r_{smooth}$), muscle usage ($r_{musc}$), joint-limit
torques ($r_{constr}$), and non-foot self-contact ($r_{self}$).
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r_t^{base} =\,
&w_{vel}r_{vel}+w_{grf}r_{grf}+w_{smooth}r_{smooth}\\
&+w_{musc}r_{musc}+w_{constr}r_{constr}+w_{self}r_{self}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For the Residual-Reflex RL controller, additional terms regularize pelvis pitch
($r_{\theta}$), pelvis pitch rate ($r_{\dot{\theta}}$), vertical
center-of-mass velocity ($r_{com}$), and residual-action changes
($r_{\Delta a}$).
\begin{equation}
r_t = r_t^{base} + w_{\theta}r_{\theta}
+w_{\dot{\theta}}r_{\dot{\theta}}+w_{com}r_{com}+w_{\Delta a}r_{\Delta a}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Tasks and Evaluation Metrics}
The nominal walking experiment evaluates whether the proposed controller
generates plausible steady gait under controlled target-speed tasks. Policies
were evaluated at target walking speeds of 0.8, 1.0, and 1.2~m/s. For each
trained policy, a 25s rollout was exported as an STO file for post-hoc gait
analysis. All detected gait cycles in the rollout were normalized to 0--100\%
gait cycle. The comparison
includes E2E-RL, DEP-RL, and Residual-Reflex RL. Four groups of metrics are
used, including task-level speed and stride measures, human-reference gait
profiles, GRF morphology, and symmetry/repeatability.

\subsubsection{Speed tracking and stride measures}
To verify that the policies satisfy the commanded locomotion task, we report
realized walking speed, absolute speed error
$|\hat{v}-v_{\mathrm{target}}|$, stride length, stride time, and the number of
analyzed cycles. Lower speed error indicates better task tracking, whereas
stride length and stride time describe the gait strategy used to achieve the
target speed. The stride measures were computed from the detected gait cycles
in each evaluated trajectory.

\subsubsection{Human-reference kinematic gait analysis}
To quantify whether the generated joint motion resembles human walking, we
compared the simulated gait-cycle profiles with human-reference gait data for
hip angle, knee angle, and ankle angle. The gray bands in
the profile figures denote lower and upper bounds derived from the reference
data. They were used only for post-hoc evaluation and were not included in the
policy reward.
For each quantity, left and right gait cycles were time-normalized
and averaged to obtain a mean simulated profile $\bar{y}(x)$, where $x$
denotes gait-cycle percentage. Let $y_{\min}(x_k)$ and $y_{\max}(x_k)$ denote
the lower and upper human-reference bounds at sample point $x_k$. The
normalized reference-range violation is
\begin{equation}
e = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
\frac{
\left[y_{\min}(x_k)-\bar{y}(x_k)\right]_+
+ \left[\bar{y}(x_k)-y_{\max}(x_k)\right]_+
}{
\max\left(0.01, y_{\max}(x_k)-y_{\min}(x_k)\right)
},
\end{equation}
where $[z]_+=\max(z,0)$.
The corresponding score is
\begin{equation}
S = 100 \cdot \mathrm{clip}(1-e,0,1).
\end{equation}
Higher scores indicate that the averaged trajectory remains closer to the
human-reference range; a score of 100 means that the mean profile stays entirely
inside the reference bounds. The kinematic average score is the mean of the hip,
knee, and ankle component scores.

\subsubsection{GRF morphology}
Because vertical GRF directly reflects stance loading and push-off behavior, we
also visualize individual left and right GRF gait cycles. This analysis is used
to assess whether the controller produces repeatable stance-phase loading,
reasonable double-support/propulsion morphology, and avoids abnormal impact
peaks. The numerical GRF score is the GRF component of the human-reference
range metric, applied separately to vertical GRF, while the cycle plots show
left--right and stride-to-stride consistency.

\subsubsection{Symmetry and repeatability}
Finally, bilateral symmetry was quantified as the root-mean-square difference
between the average left and right normalized profiles. For a gait-cycle
quantity $y$, this waveform difference is
\begin{equation}
D_y =
\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
\left(\bar{y}_{L}(x_k)-\bar{y}_{R}(x_k)\right)^2},
\end{equation}
where $y$ denotes a normalized gait-cycle waveform, $x_k$ is the $k$th
gait-cycle sample, $N$ is the number of samples in the normalized gait cycle,
and $\bar{y}_{L}$ and $\bar{y}_{R}$ are the average left- and right-side
profiles computed over the analyzed cycles. In the results, lower $D_y$
indicates more symmetric left--right motion.

Stride-to-stride repeatability was quantified using the coefficient of
variation (CV) of stride time and stride length.
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CV}_{z}=100\frac{\sigma_z}{\mu_z},
\end{equation}
where $z$ denotes either stride time or stride length, $\mu_z$ is the mean
value of $z$, and $\sigma_z$ is its standard deviation over the analyzed gait
cycles. The factor 100 expresses CV as a percentage. In the results, lower
$\mathrm{CV}_z$ indicates more repeatable gait cycles.
Accordingly, the Hip, Knee, Ankle, and GRF columns in the symmetry table report
$D_y$ for the corresponding left--right waveform pair, whereas the Time CV and
Length CV columns report $\mathrm{CV}_z$ for stride time and stride length.

Ground reaction force provides a direct view of how each controller loads and
unloads the body during stance. Residual-Reflex RL produced smoother vertical

appear visually plausible in some frames, but still interact with the ground

Residual-Reflex RL suggest that the reflex prior helps organize the stance
phase, not only the joint-angle trajectories.

\subsection{Adaptation to Plantarflexor Weakness}
\label{sec:weakness-adaptation}
This evaluation asks whether the trained residual-reflex controller remains
usable after muscle capacity changes, without updating the policy. This
directly tests state-dependent reflex modulation beyond a single
offline-optimized parameter set. Plantarflexor weakness was simulated by
reducing the maximum isometric force of the soleus muscles while keeping the
trained 1.2m/s policy fixed. The evaluated strength levels were 83--100\% of
the nominal soleus capacity. Residual-Reflex RL was compared with the
CMA-ES-optimized reflex controller under the same weakened character models.
We report rollout duration, realized speed, gait quality, and the
root-mean-square magnitude of each residual-action channel,
\begin{equation}
A_i^{\mathrm{RMS}} =
\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}a_i(t)^2},
\end{equation}
where $a_i(t)$ is the $i$th residual action and $T$ is the number of evaluated
policy steps. This magnitude measures how strongly a channel is used regardless
of sign, because a residual action may increase and decrease the corresponding
reflex parameter at different points in the gait cycle.

The comparison revealed a clear difference between state-dependent residual
modulation and the fixed optimized reflex parameters. The CMA-ES-optimized
reflex controller terminated early for all tested weakened models, whereas
Residual-Reflex RL produced full 1000-step rollouts for soleus strength levels
from 83\% to 100\%, as summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:weakness_survival}.

Table~\ref{tab:weakness_adaptation} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:weakness_speed_adaptation}
show that the residual policy did not simply force the weakened model to keep
the nominal 1.2~m/s speed. Instead, the realized speed decreased as soleus
strength was reduced, from 1.20~m/s in the nominal model to 0.95~m/s at 83\%
soleus strength. This behavior is consistent with a conservative adaptation
strategy and resembles a common compensatory response in human gait. When lower
limb force capacity is reduced, people often reduce walking speed to maintain
comfort, stability, and coordinated limb motion. Similarly, the controller
preserves stable and natural-looking walking by accepting a slower gait rather
than maintaining the target speed at the cost of instability.
Importantly, the slower gait was not simply a degraded survival behavior. The
generated joint trajectories remained close to the human-reference kinematic
profiles across the tested weakness levels, as shown in
Table~\ref{tab:weakness_kinematic_scores} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:weakness_kinematic_profiles}. Even at 83\% soleus strength, the
policy still produced a full rollout with a kinematic-average score of
89.0\%, indicating that the changed muscle model could be accommodated without
retraining while preserving a natural gait pattern.
The second part of the \suppvideo{} shows this adaptation continuously. As
plantarflexor capacity is reduced, Residual-Reflex RL retains a recognizable
stance--swing sequence and upright progression while walking more slowly. Under
the same weakened models, the CMA-ES-optimized reflex controller loses balance
and terminates. The synchronized residual-action traces further show that the
surviving motion is accompanied by ongoing modulation rather than playback of a
fixed excitation sequence.

The residual-action RMS values also show that the policy remains actively
engaged under the weakened conditions. The four channels correspond directly to
the selected reflex pathways in Table~\ref{tab:residual_actions}. Specifically, $a_1$
modulates hamstrings length feedback for hip extension and late-swing braking,
$a_2$ modulates iliopsoas length-offset feedback for hip flexion and swing
initiation, $a_3$ modulates soleus force feedback for ankle support and
propulsion, and $a_4$ modulates vasti force feedback for knee extension and
stance support. The soleus-related channel $a_3$ has the largest RMS magnitude
across the weakness range, indicating that ankle support/propulsion remains the
dominant modulated pathway even when the soleus force capacity is altered.
However, the policy does not simply increase the soleus-related action as the
muscle becomes weaker. Instead, it accepts a lower walking speed and
redistributes modulation across the other pathways. In particular, the
vasti-related channel $a_4$ is more strongly engaged under larger weakness,
which is consistent with increased reliance on knee-extension support when
ankle push-off capacity is reduced. The hamstrings channel $a_1$ and iliopsoas
channel $a_2$ vary more mildly, helping adjust late-swing braking, swing
initiation, leg placement, and step timing. The paired trends between the hip
extension/flexion channels ($a_1$, $a_2$) and the ankle/knee support channels
($a_3$, $a_4$) suggest a coordinated redistribution of reflex modulation rather
than a single-parameter compensation. These results indicate that the
four-dimensional residual action space acts as a compact adaptive interface on
top of the reflex controller. The same trained policy can be transferred across
weakened body models without retraining, while still modulating the underlying
reflex pathways online.

\subsection{Recovery from External Pushes}
\label{sec:push-recovery}
This evaluation asks whether the unchanged trained policy can restore sustained
walking after an environmental disturbance that was absent during training.
The same 1.2m/s policy used for nominal walking and weakness evaluation was
retained without further learning. Backward force pulses were applied to the
torso for 0.1s starting at 3s. We tested 75N and 100N pushes as both single pulses and
repeated pulses every 5s. Residual-Reflex RL was compared with the
CMA-ES-optimized reflex controller under identical perturbation settings.
Rollout duration and realized speed are reported to distinguish recovery of
sustained walking from a brief delayed fall.

The comparison again revealed a clear difference between the two control
representations. The CMA-ES-optimized reflex controller terminated after
approximately 4.5--4.6s for both the single and repeated 75/100N settings. In
contrast, Residual-Reflex RL produced representative full-episode recoveries
under the same 75/100N single-push conditions.

Table~\ref{tab:push_perturbation} summarizes the perturbation outcomes. Under
single backward pushes of 75 and 100N, Residual-Reflex RL maintained walking
for the full 25s evaluation window. Repeated perturbations every 5s were
substantially harder. The 75N repeated-push case still produced a full rollout,
whereas the 100N case lasted 20.1s. In comparison, the CMA-ES-optimized reflex
controller did not recover beyond roughly 4.5s in the same perturbation
settings. This pattern indicates that the learned reflex-parameter policy
provides additional recovery capacity beyond the fixed optimized reflex gains.
Together with the weakness test, these results support the central claim that
the learned residual policy provides a flexible reflex-parameter modulation
mechanism beyond offline optimized reflex gains.
Figure~\ref{fig:push_snapshots} provides a time-aligned visual comparison for
the 100N single-push condition. Before the push, both controllers produce an
upright walking configuration. After the force pulse, the CMA-ES-optimized
reflex controller progressively leans backward, loses support, and terminates
at 4.57s. Under the identical disturbance, Residual-Reflex RL reorganizes the
subsequent steps, remains upright, and continues walking for the full 25s
evaluation. The matched frames therefore distinguish sustained post-push
walking from a trajectory that only delays termination.
The final part of the \suppvideo{} visualizes the disturbance and its temporal
consequences. Residual-Reflex RL is displaced backward immediately after the
force pulse, reorganizes the following steps, and resumes periodic forward
walking in the successful trials. In contrast, the CMA-ES-optimized reflex
controller does not restore a sustained gait cycle after the same pushes. The
repeated-push sequences also show the limit of this recovery: the 75N condition
remains stable for the full rollout, whereas accumulated disturbances in the
100N condition eventually lead to termination.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
The results indicate that the main benefit of residual reflex learning is a
more structured and physiologically plausible gait pattern together with a
limited but measurable form of adaptability. In the nominal-speed data, E2E-RL
and DEP-RL can achieve successful forward walking, yet their gait-cycle profiles
may deviate substantially from reference kinematics and GRF. This observation
suggests that learned musculoskeletal gait should be assessed not only by task
completion, but also through gait-cycle kinematics, GRF morphology, bilateral
symmetry, and cycle-to-cycle repeatability.

The observed improvement is consistent with the role of the reflex prior. In
E2E-RL and DEP-RL, the policy must discover both the low-level muscle
coordination and the task-level walking strategy directly in the muscle action
space. In Residual-Reflex RL, the baseline reflex controller already provides
phase-dependent sensory feedback and stance support, while the learned policy
adjusts only a small set of interpretable reflex parameters. This restriction
does not merely reduce dimensionality; it biases exploration toward coordinated
changes in existing neuromechanical feedback pathways. The more clustered
kinematic cycles, smoother GRF profiles, and lower stride variability are
consistent with this structured action representation. The weakness and
perturbation results further clarify why learning on top of the reflex
controller is useful even when an optimized reflex baseline can walk in the
nominal condition. A CMA-ES-optimized reflex controller uses feedback, but its
parameters are selected offline and then held constant during evaluation.
Residual-Reflex RL keeps the same feedback pathways while allowing the policy to
reshape their effective gains and offsets online. This distinction becomes visible when muscle
capacity is reduced or an external push is applied, because the residual policy can
trade off speed, posture, and reflex modulation to maintain walking, whereas the
offline optimized reflex parameters are more brittle.

The present evidence also has several limitations. All policies were trained
and evaluated on one musculoskeletal character, and the nominal experiments use
separately trained fixed-speed policies rather than one controller conditioned
on a continuous speed command. The weakness study changes only soleus force
capacity, while the perturbation study considers prescribed backward torso
forces. Moreover, the reported transfer behavior demonstrates post-training
adaptation within these conditions rather than universal robustness to arbitrary
model changes or disturbances. Broader characters, randomized impairment and
perturbation distributions, and repeated training seeds are needed to establish
the generality of the learned reflex-parameter representation.


\section{Conclusion}
We presented a Residual-Reflex RL framework for musculoskeletal gait
generation. Rather than directly commanding all muscle
excitations, the learned policy modulates four gait-relevant reflex parameters
around a reflex-based controller. This formulation reduces the effective action
space and preserves a structured neuromechanical prior within the control loop.

Experiments in a Hyfydy-based musculoskeletal simulation showed that the
Residual-Reflex RL controller produced gait cycles with higher
human-reference gait scores than E2E-RL and DEP-RL at 0.8, 1.0, and 1.2~m/s.
The improvement was most evident in knee kinematics, ankle kinematics, and
vertical GRF morphology. Additional cycle-level analysis showed lower
left--right waveform differences and lower stride variability, indicating more
regular and symmetric gait patterns. Beyond nominal walking, the same trained
policy preserved full-episode walking under reduced soleus strength by adopting
a slower gait and modulating the selected reflex parameters. It also recovered
from representative external push perturbations that caused the
CMA-ES-optimized reflex controller to terminate.
These findings suggest that reflex-parameter modulation can provide a compact
and interpretable learning interface for musculoskeletal gait synthesis, where
task success must be accompanied by physiologically plausible joint motion and
contact loading. Such a framework may be useful for simulation-based studies of
human locomotion, rehabilitation-oriented controller design, and assistive
device control where both adaptability and neuromuscular interpretability are
important. Future work will extend the framework to online speed transitions,
more systematic perturbation-recovery protocols, and broader musculoskeletal
models.
