\title{\LARGE \bf
Low-Dimensional Reflex-Informed Deep Reinforcement Learning for Musculoskeletal Gait Generation
}
\author{Anonymous Authors}

\maketitle
\thispagestyle{empty}
\pagestyle{empty}

\begin{abstract}
Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has shown promise for controlling
musculoskeletal locomotion, but direct muscle-level control requires
exploration in a high-dimensional and highly redundant action space. This study
investigates Residual-Reflex RL, a low-dimensional reflex-informed DRL pipeline
for musculoskeletal gait generation. Instead of directly commanding all muscle
excitations, the proposed pipeline uses an existing
reflex-based controller as a structured neuromechanical prior inspired by
spinal sensory feedback pathways. A DRL policy then acts in a compact
reflex-parameter space, applying residual modulation to a small set of
gait-relevant feedback pathways while preserving the biomechanical structure
embedded in the reflex controller. The pipeline is validated in a Hyfydy-based planar
musculoskeletal model using target-speed walking tasks at 0.8, 1.0, and
1.2~m/s. The proposed controller is compared with two direct muscle-control
baselines: end-to-end reinforcement learning (E2E-RL) with standard policy
exploration and DEP-RL with DEP-style embodied exploration. Although E2E-RL
and DEP-RL can
generate walking motions, their normalized gait-cycle profiles show substantial
deviations from reference gait, especially in knee kinematics and vertical
ground reaction force. In contrast, the Residual-Reflex RL controller achieves
higher reference-envelope scores across the tested speeds and
produces more symmetric and repeatable gait cycles. These
results suggest that reducing the effective action space through residual
reflex modulation can improve the physiological plausibility of learned
musculoskeletal walking.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords---}Deep reinforcement learning, gait generation,
musculoskeletal simulation, reflex control, residual reinforcement learning.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Human walking emerges from the interaction of neural feedback, muscle--tendon
dynamics, skeletal mechanics, and foot--ground
contact. Physics-based musculoskeletal models are therefore attractive for
studying and synthesizing gait because they explicitly connect muscle
activation to joint motion, contact loading, and whole-body movement
\cite{anderson2001,geijtenbeek2013,lee2019,degroote2021}. However, controlling
such models remains difficult. The system is highly overactuated, muscle
dynamics are nonlinear and strongly coupled, and many different excitation
patterns can produce similar task-level motion. Consequently, a controller that
tracks a speed or completes a walking episode may still generate gait that is
irregular, asymmetric, or physiologically implausible.

Classical predictive simulation and trajectory-optimization approaches have
provided important insight into human gait mechanics and energetic objectives
\cite{anderson2001,falisse2019,degroote2021}. These methods can produce
biomechanically meaningful motions, but often require carefully designed cost
functions, contact handling, and numerical optimization over the full movement
trajectory. In parallel, muscle-based character simulation has demonstrated
that anatomically constrained actuation can improve physical realism in
synthetic locomotion \cite{geijtenbeek2013,lee2019}. These studies highlight a
central tension in musculoskeletal gait generation: rich muscle-driven models
offer physiological detail, but their control spaces are difficult to search.

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) offers a complementary route by learning
closed-loop policies through interaction with simulation. DRL has been used to
generate locomotion in physics-based characters and musculoskeletal models,
including policies that directly command muscle activations or excitations
\cite{peng2017,weng2021,devree2021,ogum2024}. Reviews and recent studies have
also emphasized that neuromechanical simulation is a promising, but still
challenging, domain for reinforcement learning because exploration, reward
design, and gait-quality evaluation become more difficult as the muscle action
space grows \cite{song2021,nowakowski2021,su2023}. In this setting, end-to-end
muscle-control policies can learn to walk, yet task reward or episode survival
alone does not guarantee human-like joint coordination, loading patterns, or
left--right symmetry.

Several lines of work therefore introduce structure into locomotion control.
Reflex-based neuromuscular controllers encode local sensory feedback pathways
and can reproduce human-like walking dynamics and muscle activities
\cite{geyer2010,song2015}. Central-pattern-generator and reflex-hybrid models
further suggest that rhythmic neural priors and sensory feedback can provide
compact mechanisms for speed regulation and gait coordination
\cite{ijspeert2008,dzeladini2014,li2024}. Recent reflex-based predictive
studies also show that a relatively small set of physiologically meaningful
control factors can explain efficient walking across speeds
\cite{koseki2024}. Nevertheless, such controllers typically depend on
carefully selected gains, thresholds, and offsets. These parameters are often
tuned manually or optimized offline, and their effects can be sensitive to the
musculoskeletal model and task condition.

Another strategy is to improve learning in the high-dimensional muscle action
space. For example, DEP-RL introduces embodied exploratory feedback for
overactuated and musculoskeletal systems while preserving direct muscle-level
control \cite{deprl2023}. This raises a related question: rather than only
improving exploration in the original muscle excitation space, can the effective
action space itself be reduced by learning at the level of structured reflex
modulation?

In this study, we address this gap by combining the structure of reflex control
with the adaptability of DRL. The goal is to avoid two extremes: hand-tuning a
fixed reflex controller, or learning all muscle excitations directly in a
high-dimensional action space. Inspired by residual reinforcement learning
\cite{residualrl2019}, the proposed method keeps the reflex controller as a
low-level neuromuscular feedback prior and trains a policy to output residual
adjustments to a small set of selected reflex parameters. DRL is therefore used
to modulate an interpretable neuromechanical controller rather than to directly
command muscle excitations.

The main contributions are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item To reduce the burden of direct muscle-level learning, we propose a
    neuro-inspired hierarchical control framework in which DRL modulates a
    reflex-based low-level controller instead of directly commanding all muscle
    excitations.
    \item To make reflex adaptation learnable and interpretable, we introduce a
    low-dimensional residual policy formulation that adjusts four gait-critical
    reflex parameters, allowing coordinated modulation of hip, knee, and ankle
    feedback pathways.
    \item To test whether task success translates into gait plausibility, we
    validate the framework against E2E-RL and DEP-RL using gait-cycle
    kinematics, vertical GRF morphology, bilateral symmetry, and stride
    repeatability. The results show more reference-consistent knee, ankle, and
    contact-loading patterns under residual reflex modulation.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section II presents the residual reflex control formulation and learning pipeline; Section III describes the controlled walking validation setup and evaluation metrics; Section IV presents and analyzes the results; and Section V concludes the paper and outlines
future work.


\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Residual-Reflex Control Architecture}
The proposed method treats the reflex controller as a structured low-level
motor prior inspired by spinal sensory feedback circuits and learns only a
compact residual modulation on top of it. As
shown in Fig.~\ref{framwork}, the musculoskeletal model returns proprioceptive
and kinematic states to a high-level DRL policy. The policy outputs a
low-dimensional residual action, which modifies selected reflex parameters
before the reflex controller generates muscle excitations. The resulting muscle
excitations are then transformed into skeletal motion and ground reaction forces
by the musculoskeletal simulator. This hierarchy changes the role of DRL from
direct muscle excitation control to reflex-parameter modulation.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{framwork.png}}
\caption{Low-dimensional reflex-informed DRL pipeline. The learned policy does
not directly command all muscle excitations. Instead, it modulates selected
parameters of a neural-feedback-inspired reflex controller, which then produces
muscle-level stimulation for the
musculoskeletal model.}
\label{framwork}
\end{figure}

Let $\mathbf{s}_t$ denote the state observed by the policy at time $t$. The
state contains muscle-level signals, foot states, whole-body kinematics, and
joint-space variables:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{s}_t = [
&\mathbf{l}_t,\dot{\mathbf{l}}_t,\mathbf{f}_t,\mathbf{e}_t,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t,\mathbf{p}_{feet,t},\\
&\mathbf{q}_t,\dot{\mathbf{q}}_t,\theta_t,\dot{\theta}_t],
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{l}$, $\dot{\mathbf{l}}$, and $\mathbf{f}$ are muscle fiber
lengths, velocities, and forces; $\mathbf{e}$ and $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ are
muscle excitations and activations; $\mathbf{p}_{feet}$ represents foot
positions; $\mathbf{q}$ and $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$ are joint angles and angular
velocities; and $\theta$ is the trunk pitch angle.

\subsection{Reflex Controller}
The low-level controller is a phase-dependent reflex controller inspired by
neuromuscular feedback pathways, including muscle-spindle-like length and
velocity feedback and Golgi-tendon-organ-like force feedback. The gait cycle is divided into stance,
liftoff, swing, and landing-related subphases. In each subphase, sparse
reflex pathways map sensory feedback to target muscle stimulation. For a target
muscle $i$ and a source muscle $j$, the controller includes length, velocity,
and force feedback terms:
\begin{align}
u_{L,i\leftarrow j} &= k_{L,i\leftarrow j}
\max(0,\tilde{l}_j(t-t_D)-\tilde{l}_{0,i\leftarrow j}),\\
u_{V,i\leftarrow j} &= k_{V,i\leftarrow j}
\max(0,\tilde{v}_j(t-t_D)-\tilde{v}_{0,i\leftarrow j}),\\
u_{F,i\leftarrow j} &= k_{F,i\leftarrow j}
(\tilde{f}_j(t-t_D)-\tilde{f}_{0,i\leftarrow j}),
\end{align}
where $\tilde{l}$, $\tilde{v}$, and $\tilde{f}$ are normalized muscle length,
velocity, and force; $k_L$, $k_V$, and $k_F$ are reflex gains; and $t_D$ is a
sensorimotor delay. A reflex-like postural feedback term is also used to
couple trunk pitch errors to selected lower-limb muscles:
\begin{equation}
u_{\text{PD},i}=k_{P,i}(\theta(t-t_D)-\theta_{0,i})+
k_{D,i}\dot{\theta}(t-t_D).
\end{equation}
The total stimulation of muscle $i$ is the sum of feedforward, local feedback,
and postural feedback components:
\begin{equation}
u_i = u_{C,i}+u_{\text{PD},i}
+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}(u_{L,i\leftarrow j}
+u_{V,i\leftarrow j}+u_{F,i\leftarrow j}).
\end{equation}
This reflex structure provides immediate sensory feedback and a biomechanical
inductive bias before policy learning is applied.

\subsection{Residual Action Space}
Instead of allowing the policy to directly command all muscle excitations, the
proposed controller uses a four-dimensional residual action
$\mathbf{a}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{4}$. Each action component modulates one selected
reflex parameter around its nominal value:
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)=p_{i,0}(1+\lambda a_i(t)),
\end{equation}
where $p_{i,0}$ is the nominal reflex parameter and $\lambda$ is the action
scale. In the present implementation, $\lambda=0.5$. Thus $a_i(t)=0$ recovers
the underlying reflex controller, while nonzero actions produce state-dependent
residual modulation.

The pathway labels in Table~\ref{tab:residual_actions} follow the controller

These four parameters were selected because they act on gait-critical pathways:
hip flexion/extension for leg swing and placement, knee extension for stance
support, and ankle plantarflexion for support and propulsion. The purpose of
this selection is not to claim global optimality of the four parameters, but to
test whether a small and interpretable reflex-parameter action space can guide
DRL toward more natural gait than direct muscle-level control.

\subsection{Policy Learning}
The policy is trained with Maximum a Posteriori Policy Optimization (MPO)
\cite{mpo2018}, an off-policy actor--critic algorithm for continuous control.
The same optimizer is used across the compared policies. Therefore, the main experimental
comparison isolates the control representation: low-dimensional
reflex-parameter modulation versus direct muscle-level action, with and without
DEP-style exploratory feedback.

At each time step, the policy samples an action from a Gaussian action
distribution conditioned on $\mathbf{s}_t$. The action is applied to the
controller, the simulator advances one step, and the transition
$(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t,r_t,\mathbf{s}_{t+1})$ is stored for off-policy
updates. The base reward combines target-speed tracking ($r_{vel}$) with
regularization terms for excessive ground reaction force ($r_{grf}$), abrupt
excitation changes ($r_{smooth}$), muscle usage ($r_{musc}$), joint-limit
torques ($r_{constr}$), and non-foot self-contact ($r_{self}$):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r_t^{base} =\,
&w_{vel}r_{vel}+w_{grf}r_{grf}+w_{smooth}r_{smooth}\\
&+w_{musc}r_{musc}+w_{constr}r_{constr}+w_{self}r_{self}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For the Residual-Reflex RL controller, additional terms regularize pelvis pitch
($r_{\theta}$), pelvis pitch rate ($r_{\dot{\theta}}$), vertical
center-of-mass velocity ($r_{com}$), and residual-action changes
($r_{\Delta a}$):
\begin{equation}
r_t = r_t^{base} + w_{\theta}r_{\theta}
+w_{\dot{\theta}}r_{\dot{\theta}}+w_{com}r_{com}+w_{\Delta a}r_{\Delta a}.
\end{equation}
\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Environments and Tasks}\label{sec:experiments}
\subsubsection{Simulation Environment}
The experiments were conducted in a two-dimensional planar musculoskeletal
model implemented with the Hyfydy physics engine \cite{hyfydy}. The model
contains the pelvis, trunk, and bilateral lower-limb segments, with
sagittal-plane hip, knee, and ankle motion. Actuation is provided by 18
Hill-type muscles, corresponding to nine muscle groups per leg: tibialis
anterior (TA), soleus (SOL), gastrocnemius (GAS), vasti (VAS), biceps femoris
short head (BIF), iliopsoas (ILI), hamstrings (HAM), gluteus maximus (GLU), and
rectus femoris (FEM). Foot--ground contact elements are used to compute
vertical ground reaction forces for gait analysis.

\subsubsection{Compared Methods}
Three DRL methods were compared. \emph{E2E-RL} denotes an
end-to-end reinforcement learning policy that directly outputs muscle-level
control actions, following the direct muscle-control setting
commonly used in musculoskeletal DRL studies
\cite{weng2021,devree2021,ogum2024}. \emph{DEP-RL}
uses the same direct muscle-level action space, but adds DEP-style embodied
exploration \cite{deprl2023}. The proposed \emph{Residual-Reflex RL} controller learns
four-dimensional residual actions that modulate the selected reflex parameters
in Table~\ref{tab:residual_actions}. All policies were trained with MPO-based
off-policy reinforcement learning on the same musculoskeletal model.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}
Policies were evaluated at three target walking speeds: 0.8, 1.0, and
1.2~m/s. For each trained policy, a representative walking trajectory was
exported as an STO file. All detected gait cycles were normalized to
0--100\% gait cycle and analyzed using the following metrics.

\subsubsection{Speed tracking and stride measures}
To verify that the policies satisfy the commanded locomotion task, we report
realized walking speed, absolute speed error
$|\hat{v}-v_{\mathrm{target}}|$, stride length, stride time, and the number of
analyzed cycles. Lower speed error indicates better task tracking, whereas
stride length and stride time describe the gait strategy used to achieve the
target speed. The stride measures were computed from the detected gait cycles
in each evaluated trajectory.

\subsubsection{Reference-envelope gait analysis}
To quantify whether the generated motion follows reference gait-cycle shapes,
we computed reference-envelope scores for pelvic tilt, hip angle, knee angle,
ankle angle, and vertical GRF. The gray reference envelopes in the profile
figures denote lower and upper bounds from the post-hoc reference-envelope data.
They were used only for evaluation and were not included in the policy reward.
For each quantity, left and right gait cycles were time-normalized
and averaged to obtain a mean simulated profile $\bar{y}(x)$, where $x$
denotes gait-cycle percentage. Let $y_{\min}(x_k)$ and $y_{\max}(x_k)$ denote
the lower and upper reference-envelope values at sample point $x_k$. The
normalized envelope violation is
\begin{equation}
e = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
\frac{
\left[y_{\min}(x_k)-\bar{y}(x_k)\right]_+
+ \left[\bar{y}(x_k)-y_{\max}(x_k)\right]_+
}{
\max\left(0.01, y_{\max}(x_k)-y_{\min}(x_k)\right)
},
\end{equation}
where $[z]_+=\max(z,0)$.
The corresponding score is
\begin{equation}
S = 100 \cdot \mathrm{clip}(1-e,0,1).
\end{equation}
Higher scores indicate that the averaged trajectory remains closer to the
reference envelope; a score of 100 means that the mean profile stays entirely
inside the envelope. The overall gait score is the average of the pelvic, hip,
knee, ankle, and GRF component scores.

\subsubsection{GRF morphology}
Because vertical GRF directly reflects stance loading and push-off behavior, we
also visualize individual left and right GRF gait cycles. This analysis is used
to assess whether the controller produces repeatable stance-phase loading,
reasonable double-support/propulsion morphology, and avoids abnormal impact
peaks. The numerical GRF score is the GRF component of the reference-envelope
metric above, while the cycle plots show left--right and stride-to-stride
consistency.

\subsubsection{Symmetry and repeatability}
Finally, bilateral symmetry was quantified as the root-mean-square difference
between the average left and right normalized profiles. For a gait-cycle
quantity $y$, this waveform difference is
\begin{equation}
D_y =
\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
\left(\bar{y}_{L}(x_k)-\bar{y}_{R}(x_k)\right)^2},
\end{equation}
where $y$ denotes a normalized gait-cycle waveform, $x_k$ is the $k$th
gait-cycle sample, $N$ is the number of samples in the normalized gait cycle,
and $\bar{y}_{L}$ and $\bar{y}_{R}$ are the average left- and right-side
profiles computed over the analyzed cycles. In the results, lower $D_y$
indicates more symmetric left--right motion.

Stride-to-stride repeatability was quantified using the coefficient of
variation (CV) of stride time and stride length:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CV}_{z}=100\frac{\sigma_z}{\mu_z},
\end{equation}
where $z$ denotes either stride time or stride length, $\mu_z$ is the mean
value of $z$, and $\sigma_z$ is its standard deviation over the analyzed gait
cycles. The factor 100 expresses CV as a percentage. In the results, lower
$\mathrm{CV}_z$ indicates more repeatable gait cycles.

\section{Results and Analysis}
\label{sec:results-and-analysis}
The comparison is designed to separate task completion from gait quality. We
therefore first verify that all controllers generate forward walking near the
commanded speeds, and then examine whether the resulting gait cycles preserve
reference-like kinematics, contact loading, bilateral symmetry, and
cycle-to-cycle repeatability.

\subsection{Speed Tracking and Stride Measures}
All three methods were able to generate sustained forward walking in
the controlled speed tasks. Table~\ref{tab:spatiotemporal_metrics} shows that Residual-Reflex RL tracked the commanded
speed most closely at all three target speeds, with absolute speed errors of
0.01, 0.05, and 0.01~m/s. E2E-RL and DEP-RL also produced
walking trials, but their realized speeds and stride measures varied more across
conditions. For example, E2E-RL overshot the 1.0~m/s target, whereas DEP-RL
overshot both the 1.0 and 1.2~m/s targets. These task-level results establish
that the subsequent comparisons are made among controllers that can complete the
basic walking objective, rather than between walking and failure cases. The
stride measures also suggest different gait strategies: E2E-RL tends to use
shorter and faster steps at lower speed, while DEP-RL and Residual-Reflex RL use
longer strides. Therefore, speed tracking alone is not sufficient to determine
which controller generates the more physiologically plausible gait.

\subsection{Reference-Envelope Gait Analysis}
The gait-cycle scores reveal a clearer separation between the three methods.
As listed in Table~\ref{tab:gait_similarity}, Residual-Reflex RL achieved the highest
overall reference-envelope score, with an average score of 89.9\%,
compared with 64.9\% for E2E-RL and 51.3\% for DEP-RL. The main differences appear in the knee,
ankle, and vertical GRF components, where the Residual-Reflex RL controller more
consistently preserved reference-like joint coordination and stance-phase
loading. E2E-RL retained relatively high hip scores, and in some cases high
ankle scores, but its knee and GRF scores were much less consistent. DEP-RL did
not improve this pattern in the present validation data; its overall score was
lower than E2E-RL at 1.0 and 1.2~m/s, mainly because of poor GRF agreement.
Scores close to zero indicate that the averaged profile lies outside the
reference envelope over most of the normalized gait cycle, rather than a small
localized deviation.
This contrast is important because E2E-RL and DEP-RL can complete the
speed-tracking task, yet their gait cycles can still deviate substantially from
reference kinematics and contact dynamics.
The bar chart in Fig.~\ref{fig:gait_score_bars} makes this contrast visible:
Residual-Reflex RL improves the overall score mainly by lifting the knee,
ankle, and GRF components, rather than by only matching one isolated joint. In
contrast, E2E-RL can obtain a high hip score while still failing in ankle or GRF
components, showing that a single joint profile can look acceptable even when
the full gait pattern is not reference-consistent.

\subsection{GRF Morphology}
Ground reaction force provides a direct view of how each controller loads and
unloads the body during stance. Residual-Reflex RL produced smoother vertical
GRF profiles with a more reference-consistent double-support pattern across the
tested speeds, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:grf_morphology}. E2E-RL showed
abnormal or inconsistent loading despite visually plausible joint postures in
some snapshots; this explains why its GRF scores were much lower than its hip
or ankle scores. DEP-RL produced even larger early-stance peaks and
stride-to-stride fluctuations at 1.0 and 1.2~m/s, leading to near-zero GRF
agreement in those cases. In the GRF metric, a score of 0.0 means that the mean
force profile stays outside the reference envelope after normalization, which
matches the large impact-like peaks and irregular unloading visible in the DEP-RL
panels. Residual-Reflex RL avoided these sharp loading transients and achieved
the highest GRF scores at all three speeds.
This is an important failure mode observed in E2E-RL and DEP-RL: the limbs can
appear visually plausible in some frames, but still interact with the ground
through abrupt or poorly timed contact forces. The smoother GRF curves of
Residual-Reflex RL suggest that the reflex prior helps organize the stance
phase, not only the joint-angle trajectories.

\subsection{Symmetry and Repeatability}
The residual reflex representation also improved the regularity of the generated
gait cycles. Table~\ref{tab:symmetry_variability} shows that Residual-Reflex RL
consistently produced lower left--right waveform differences and lower stride
time/length coefficients of variation than E2E-RL and DEP-RL. The largest
differences occurred in ankle symmetry and stride variability, where the
direct muscle-control methods produced visibly less repeatable cycles. These
metrics support the visual observation that residual reflex modulation yields a
more bilaterally symmetric and repeatable gait pattern, rather than merely
producing occasional successful walking cycles.
For example, at 1.2~m/s the stride time CV and stride length CV of
Residual-Reflex RL were both below 1\%, whereas E2E-RL and DEP-RL showed larger
cycle-to-cycle variation. This means that the proposed method did not only
produce a good average gait cycle; it also generated more consistent steps
throughout the analyzed trajectory.

\subsection{Qualitative Gait Visualization}
The qualitative snapshots in Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative_snapshots} give an
intuitive view of the same result at the body posture level. E2E-RL appears
reasonable at first glance: the trunk remains upright, and the 0--100\% sequence
contains recognizable stance and swing postures. This visual plausibility is
consistent with its relatively high hip and ankle scores at 1.2~m/s. However,
the motion is less convincing when interpreted together with the GRF result.
The limb postures do not necessarily imply a smooth transfer of body weight, and
the low GRF score indicates that the contact loading pattern is not
reference-consistent.

DEP-RL shows the clearest qualitative deviations. Around 25\% of the sampled
cycle, the body posture appears pitched forward and the swing leg is lifted with
a less natural knee--ankle configuration. Around 75\%, the limb configuration is
again less coordinated, with the leg appearing to advance without a smooth
stance-to-swing transition. These snapshots are consistent with the lower knee,
ankle, and GRF scores of DEP-RL, and with the irregular force profiles observed
in Fig.~\ref{fig:grf_morphology}. In contrast, Residual-Reflex RL maintains a
more upright trunk and a more gradual progression from stance to swing across
the sampled cycle. Its posture sequence is not only visually smoother, but also
supported quantitatively by high joint-angle scores and high GRF agreement.

\subsection{Discussion}
The results indicate that the main benefit of residual reflex learning is a
more structured and physiologically plausible gait pattern. In the current
data, E2E-RL and DEP-RL can achieve successful forward walking, yet their
gait-cycle profiles may deviate substantially from reference kinematics and
GRF. This observation suggests that learned musculoskeletal gait should be
assessed not only by task completion, but also through gait-cycle kinematics,
GRF morphology, bilateral symmetry, and cycle-to-cycle repeatability.

The observed improvement is consistent with the role of the reflex prior. In
E2E-RL and DEP-RL, the policy must discover both the low-level muscle
coordination and the task-level walking strategy directly in the muscle action
space. In Residual-Reflex RL, the baseline reflex controller already provides
phase-dependent sensory feedback and stance support, while the learned policy
adjusts only a small set of interpretable reflex parameters. This restriction
does not merely reduce dimensionality; it biases exploration toward coordinated
changes in existing neuromechanical feedback pathways. The more clustered
kinematic cycles, smoother GRF profiles, and lower stride variability are
consistent with this structured action representation.


\section{Conclusion}
We presented a Residual-Reflex RL pipeline for musculoskeletal gait
generation. Rather than directly commanding all muscle
excitations, the learned policy modulates four selected reflex parameters
around a reflex-based controller. This formulation reduces the effective action
space and preserves a structured neuromechanical prior within the control loop.

As an initial validation, experiments in a Hyfydy-based planar musculoskeletal
model showed that the Residual-Reflex RL controller produced gait cycles with
higher reference-envelope scores than E2E-RL and DEP-RL at
0.8, 1.0, and 1.2~m/s.
The improvement was most evident in knee kinematics, ankle kinematics, and
vertical GRF morphology. Additional cycle-level analysis showed lower
left--right waveform differences and lower stride variability, indicating more
regular and symmetric gait patterns.

The results support the view that successful walking alone is not sufficient
for evaluating learned musculoskeletal locomotion. While the present validation
focuses on fixed-speed walking, future work will extend the evaluation to online
speed transitions and controlled perturbation-recovery tests.
