\begin{abstract}
Musculoskeletal gait generation is important for studying human locomotion,
developing assistive systems, and building bio-inspired robots. These
applications require controllers that can coordinate many redundant muscles
while preserving natural joint motion, realistic contact loading, and
adaptability to changes in the body or environment. However,
learning muscle-driven gait controllers remains challenging because direct
muscle-level deep reinforcement learning (DRL) policies typically require
exploration in a
high-dimensional and redundant action space, while traditional
optimization-based approaches often produce fixed controller parameters that do
not readily adapt to changes in the body or environment. This study proposes
Residual-Reflex RL, a low-dimensional and interpretable reflex-informed DRL
framework that treats a phase-dependent reflex controller as a neuromechanical
prior and learns residual modulation of selected reflex pathways. Instead of
directly commanding all muscles, the policy adjusts four gait-relevant reflex
parameters associated with hip swing, ankle propulsion, and stance support.
Experiments with a musculoskeletal model show that the proposed controller produces
human-like, stable, and repeatable gait, while adapting to plantarflexor weakness
and external push perturbations without retraining. These findings demonstrate
that the Residual-Reflex-Informed DRL framework can place learning at an
interpretable reflex-parameter level, enabling adaptive muscle-driven gait
generation without abandoning neuromuscular feedback structure.
\end{abstract}

\keywords{Deep reinforcement learning, gait generation, musculoskeletal

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Generating natural, stable, and adaptable gait in muscle-driven bodies is a
central problem for simulation-based human locomotion research, rehabilitation
controller design, and assistive-device development. In these settings, success
cannot be measured only by whether the model moves forward. A useful gait
controller should also produce physiologically plausible joint coordination,
smooth contact loading, interpretable neuromuscular behavior, and the ability to
cope with changes in the body or environment. Physics-based musculoskeletal
models provide a powerful testbed for this goal because they explicitly connect
muscle activation, muscle--tendon dynamics, skeletal motion, and foot--ground
contact \cite{anderson2001,geijtenbeek2013,lee2019,degroote2021,uchida2020}.
The same properties also make them difficult to control because the system is
nonlinear, overactuated, and highly redundant, so many muscle-excitation
patterns can complete a walking task while producing very different gait
quality.

Predictive simulation \cite{anderson2001,ackermann2010,geijtenbeek2019},
trajectory optimization and direct collocation \cite{falisse2019}, and
musculoskeletal optimal control \cite{dembia2020} provide one major route for
musculoskeletal gait generation. These methods can generate dynamically feasible
motions under physical constraints and have provided important insight into gait
mechanics, energetic objectives, and pathological walking
\cite{degroote2021,wang2010,wang2012,song2018}. Software frameworks have made
predictive musculoskeletal simulation increasingly practical
\cite{geijtenbeek2019,dembia2020}, but the resulting problem is still usually
solved as an offline optimization over complete trajectories.
Such approaches often require carefully designed cost functions and substantial
computation, and the optimized motion does not necessarily become a feedback
policy that can respond online to perturbations, muscle-capacity changes, or
other model variations.

A second route introduces biological structure directly into the controller.
Central pattern generator (CPG), muscle synergies, and reflex-based
neuromuscular controllers reduce the control problem by exploiting modular and
feedback-driven organization in biological locomotion
\cite{ijspeert2008,davella2003,meyer2016,geyer2010,song2015,dzeladini2014,li2024}.
Such controllers are attractive because they are compact, interpretable, and
closed-loop, with sensory feedback directly influencing muscle stimulation.
Recent reflex-based and biologically motivated studies further show that
physiologically meaningful control factors can support efficient and robust
walking across speeds, perturbations, and model variants
\cite{batts2015,koseki2024,schumacher2025}.
However, the gains, thresholds, and offsets in these controllers are often
selected manually or optimized offline. Once fixed, they may lack a
policy-level mechanism for changing reflex parameters online when muscle
capacity is altered or an external disturbance is applied.

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) offers a complementary strength by learning a
state-dependent policy through interaction with the simulated body. DRL has
generated locomotion in physics-based characters and musculoskeletal models,
including end-to-end policies that directly output muscle activations or
excitations \cite{peng2017,peng2018,weng2021,devree2021,ogum2024,schumacher2025}. This
adaptability, however, comes with a difficult action-representation problem.
Direct muscle-level control exposes the learner to a high-dimensional and
redundant action space. Exploration and reward design become harder as the
number of muscles increases, and successful episode completion alone does not
guarantee human-like joint coordination, contact loading, or left--right
symmetry \cite{song2021,nowakowski2021,su2023}. Methods such as DEP-RL improve
exploration in overactuated and musculoskeletal systems, but they still operate
through direct muscle-level actions and can produce gait patterns whose
kinematics or contact loading deviate from human walking references
\cite{deprl2023}.

Taken together, these studies reveal a gap between structured but fixed
neuromuscular control and adaptive but high-dimensional direct muscle learning.
Offline optimization and structured neuromuscular controllers encode useful
mechanics and feedback pathways, but their parameters are often fixed after
optimization. End-to-end DRL can learn state-dependent behavior, but direct
muscle-level policies still require exploration in a high-dimensional and
redundant action space. This motivates a compact and interpretable control
representation that can remain adaptive after training. Human motor control
provides a biological motivation for such a representation.
Locomotion is not learned from scratch as an arbitrary mapping from state to
every muscle excitation. Fast spinal reflex pathways provide a low-level
feedback substrate, while development and interaction with the environment shape
how higher-level neural systems regulate and adapt these existing circuits.
Inspired by this organization, this study treats a reflex controller as a
low-level neuromechanical prior and trains a DRL policy to apply residual
modulation to a small number of gait-relevant reflex parameters. This formulation
places learning at the reflex-parameter level, preserving the feedback structure
of the underlying controller while enabling state-dependent adaptation without
exploring the full muscle-excitation space.

The main contributions are listed below.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We formulate musculoskeletal gait generation as residual modulation of
    reflex pathways rather than direct muscle-excitation synthesis, changing the
    learning problem from high-dimensional muscle control to low-dimensional
    reflex-parameter control.
    \item We develop a four-dimensional residual-reflex action space grounded in
    gait-critical hip, knee, and ankle feedback pathways, providing an
    interpretable interface between learned actions and neuromechanical function.
    \item We integrate this interface with off-policy actor--critic learning, so
    the policy can reshape selected reflex gains and offsets online while the
    low-level reflex controller preserves phase-dependent sensory feedback.
    \item We validate the framework in nominal walking, plantarflexor weakness,
    and external push perturbation tests, comparing against direct muscle-action
    DRL baselines and a CMA-ES-optimized reflex baseline to examine gait
    plausibility, repeatability, and the ability of the trained policy to
    accommodate altered body and environment conditions without retraining.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section II presents the residual reflex control formulation and learning framework; Section III describes the controlled walking validation setup and evaluation metrics; Section IV presents and analyzes the results; and Section V concludes the paper and outlines
future work.


\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Framework Design Principle}
The central design choice is to place learning at the level of reflex
modulation rather than at the level of direct muscle excitation. The policy does
not replace the reflex controller; it learns how to reshape it. In this
formulation, the reflex controller remains responsible for fast phase-dependent
sensory feedback, while the DRL policy provides state-dependent residual
adjustments to a small set of reflex parameters. This separates two roles that
are entangled in direct muscle-action policies. These roles are the low-level
generation of biomechanically grounded muscle stimulation and the higher-level adaptation of
gait-relevant feedback pathways.

This design has three practical consequences. First, the action space is reduced
from an 18-dimensional muscle-control vector to a four-dimensional
reflex-parameter vector. Second, each action channel retains a functional
interpretation through the reflex pathway it modulates. Third, because the
policy acts on feedback parameters rather than on an open-loop excitation
pattern, the same trained policy can still respond to altered sensory and
mechanical states through the underlying reflex structure.

\subsection{Residual-Reflex Framework}
As shown in Fig.~\ref{framwork}, the framework is organized into three modules.
Module (a) performs the agent--environment rollout, in which the current policy
$\pi(\mathbf{s};\theta_{\mathrm{now}})$ maps the state $\mathbf{s}_t$ to a
residual action $\mathbf{a}_t$ and collects the transition
$(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t,r_t,\mathbf{s}_{t+1})$. Module (b) is the
physiological environment, where the residual-reflex controller converts the
action into modulation of selected reflex parameters, generates muscle
stimulation, and drives the musculoskeletal model. Module (c) is the
actor--critic learning module, where rollout transitions are stored in the
replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$ and used to train the critic
$q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a};\omega)$ and update the policy
$\pi(\mathbf{s};\theta_{\mathrm{new}})$ through off-policy Maximum a Posteriori
Policy Optimization (MPO) \cite{mpo2018}.

In module (a), let $\mathbf{s}_t$ denote the observation received by the policy
at time $t$.
In this implementation, $\mathbf{s}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{121}$. It contains
muscle-level sensory and activation signals from the 18 Hill-type
muscle--tendon actuators,
foot-relative positions, body orientation and angular velocity, generalized
coordinates and velocities.
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathbf{s}_t = [
&\mathbf{l}_t,\dot{\mathbf{l}}_t,\mathbf{f}_t,\mathbf{e}_t,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_t,\mathbf{p}_{feet,t},
\boldsymbol{\psi}_t,\boldsymbol{\omega}_t,\\
&\mathbf{q}_t,\dot{\mathbf{q}}_t],
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{l}$, $\dot{\mathbf{l}}$, and $\mathbf{f}$ are muscle fiber
lengths, velocities, and forces; $\mathbf{e}$ and $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ are
muscle excitations and activations; $\mathbf{p}_{feet}$ represents foot
positions relative to the body; $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}$
are body orientation and angular velocity; $\mathbf{q}$ and $\dot{\mathbf{q}}$
are generalized coordinates and velocities.

\subsection{Phase-Dependent Reflex Controller}
Module (b) contains the musculoskeletal model and the residual-reflex-informed
controller. The low-level controller is a phase-dependent reflex controller inspired by
neuromuscular feedback pathways, including muscle-spindle-like length and
velocity feedback and Golgi-tendon-organ-like force feedback. The gait cycle is
divided into five controller phases, namely EarlyStance, LateStance, Liftoff, Swing,
and Landing. In each phase, sparse
reflex pathways map sensory feedback to target muscle stimulation. For a target
muscle $i$ and a source muscle $j$, where $i\leftarrow j$ denotes feedback from
source muscle $j$ to target muscle $i$, the controller includes length,
velocity, and force feedback terms.
\begin{align}
u_{L,i\leftarrow j} &= k_{L,i\leftarrow j}
\max(0,\tilde{l}_j(t-t_D)-\tilde{l}_{0,i\leftarrow j}),\\
u_{V,i\leftarrow j} &= k_{V,i\leftarrow j}
\max(0,\tilde{v}_j(t-t_D)-\tilde{v}_{0,i\leftarrow j}),\\
u_{F,i\leftarrow j} &= k_{F,i\leftarrow j}
(\tilde{f}_j(t-t_D)-\tilde{f}_{0,i\leftarrow j}),
\end{align}
Here $u_{L,i\leftarrow j}$, $u_{V,i\leftarrow j}$, and
$u_{F,i\leftarrow j}$ are the stimulation contributions from length, velocity,
and force feedback, respectively. The variables $\tilde{l}_j$, $\tilde{v}_j$,
and $\tilde{f}_j$ denote normalized length, velocity, and force of the source
muscle. The offsets $\tilde{l}_{0,i\leftarrow j}$,
$\tilde{v}_{0,i\leftarrow j}$, and $\tilde{f}_{0,i\leftarrow j}$ define the
corresponding feedback thresholds, while $k_{L,i\leftarrow j}$,
$k_{V,i\leftarrow j}$, and $k_{F,i\leftarrow j}$ are the corresponding reflex
gains. The term $t_D$ is a sensorimotor delay. A reflex-like postural feedback
term is also used to couple trunk pitch errors to selected lower-limb muscles.
\begin{equation}
u_{\text{PD},i}=k_{P,i}(\theta(t-t_D)-\theta_{0,i})+
k_{D,i}\dot{\theta}(t-t_D).
\end{equation}
Here $\theta$ and $\dot{\theta}$ are the delayed trunk pitch angle and angular
velocity, $\theta_{0,i}$ is the target pitch offset for muscle $i$, and $k_{P,i}$
and $k_{D,i}$ are the proportional and derivative postural-feedback gains.
The total stimulation of muscle $i$ is the sum of feedforward, local feedback,
and postural feedback components.
\begin{equation}
u_i = u_{C,i}+u_{\text{PD},i}
+\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}(u_{L,i\leftarrow j}
+u_{V,i\leftarrow j}+u_{F,i\leftarrow j}).
\end{equation}
This reflex structure provides immediate sensory feedback and a biomechanical
inductive bias before policy learning is applied.

\subsection{Four-Dimensional Reflex-Parameter Action Space}
Instead of allowing the policy in module (a) to directly command all muscle excitations, the
proposed controller uses a four-dimensional residual action
$\mathbf{a}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{4}$. Each action component modulates one selected
reflex parameter around its nominal value.
\begin{equation}
p_i(t)=p_{i,0}(1+\lambda a_i(t)),
\label{eq:residual_scaling}
\end{equation}
where $p_{i,0}$ is the nominal reflex parameter and $\lambda$ is the action
scale. In the present implementation, $\lambda=0.5$. Thus $a_i(t)=0$ recovers
the underlying reflex controller, while nonzero actions produce state-dependent
residual modulation.

Thus, $a_1$ modulates hamstrings length feedback for hip extension and

including hip flexion/extension for leg swing and placement, knee extension for

residual action space concentrates policy exploration on biomechanically
meaningful feedback pathways while retaining an interpretable link between
learned actions and neuromuscular function.

\subsection{Actor--Critic Learning}
Module (c) implements the off-policy actor--critic learning loop shown in
Fig.~\ref{framwork}(c). The actor and critic are multilayer perceptrons with two
hidden layers of 256 units and ReLU activations. The actor receives the
121-dimensional observation $\mathbf{s}_t$ and parameterizes the Gaussian rollout
policy $\pi(\mathbf{s};\theta_{\mathrm{now}})$ in module (a). For
Residual-Reflex RL, its output is the mean and standard deviation of a
four-dimensional residual-action distribution,
$\mathbf{a}_t\in[-1,1]^4$, corresponding to the reflex parameters in
Table~\ref{tab:residual_actions}. The critic receives the observation--action
pair and outputs the scalar state--action value
$q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a};\omega)$, where $\omega$ denotes critic parameters.

Transitions collected during rollout,
$(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t,r_t,\mathbf{s}_{t+1})$, are stored in the replay
buffer $\mathcal{D}$. MPO trains the critic from replayed transitions and updates
the actor from the rollout policy $\pi(\mathbf{s};\theta_{\mathrm{now}})$ to the
new policy $\pi(\mathbf{s};\theta_{\mathrm{new}})$. Conceptually, the actor
update increases the expected critic value while constraining the change from
the rollout policy,
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\theta_{\mathrm{new}} = \arg\max_{\theta}\;&
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}\sim\mathcal{D},\,\mathbf{a}\sim\pi(\mathbf{s};\theta)}
\left[q(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a};\omega)\right]\\
\mathrm{s.t.}\;&
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}\sim\mathcal{D}}
\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(
\pi(\mathbf{s};\theta)\,\|\,\pi(\mathbf{s};\theta_{\mathrm{now}})
\right)\right]\le\epsilon .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The KL constraint limits abrupt policy changes during continuous-control
learning. During training, actions are sampled from the stochastic policy for
exploration; during deterministic evaluation, the policy mean is used. The
baseline controllers used for comparison are defined in the experimental
setting.

The reward $r_t$ stored in each transition defines the learning objective used
by the actor--critic update. The base reward combines target-speed tracking
($r_{vel}$) with
regularization terms for excessive ground reaction force ($r_{grf}$), abrupt
excitation changes ($r_{smooth}$), muscle usage ($r_{musc}$), joint-limit
torques ($r_{constr}$), and non-foot self-contact ($r_{self}$).
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
r_t^{base} =\,
&w_{vel}r_{vel}+w_{grf}r_{grf}+w_{smooth}r_{smooth}\\
&+w_{musc}r_{musc}+w_{constr}r_{constr}+w_{self}r_{self}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
For the Residual-Reflex RL controller, additional terms regularize pelvis pitch
($r_{\theta}$), pelvis pitch rate ($r_{\dot{\theta}}$), vertical
center-of-mass velocity ($r_{com}$), and residual-action changes
($r_{\Delta a}$).
\begin{equation}
r_t = r_t^{base} + w_{\theta}r_{\theta}
+w_{\dot{\theta}}r_{\dot{\theta}}+w_{com}r_{com}+w_{\Delta a}r_{\Delta a}.
\end{equation}
\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setting}
The experiments were conducted in a Hyfydy-based musculoskeletal simulation
\cite{hyfydy}. The model contains the pelvis, trunk, and bilateral lower-limb
segments, with nine generalized coordinates comprising pelvis translation and
orientation together with bilateral hip, knee, and ankle joint rotations.
Actuation is provided by 18 Hill-type muscle--tendon actuators, corresponding to nine muscle
groups per leg: tibialis anterior (TA), soleus (SOL), gastrocnemius (GAS),
vasti (VAS), biceps femoris short head (BIF), iliopsoas (ILI), hamstrings
(HAM), gluteus maximus (GLU), and rectus femoris (FEM). Foot--ground contact
elements are used to compute vertical ground reaction forces (GRFs) for gait
analysis.

Four controllers were considered across the validation experiments.
E2E-RL denotes an end-to-end reinforcement learning policy that directly
outputs muscle-level control actions, following the direct muscle-control
setting commonly used in musculoskeletal DRL studies
\cite{weng2021,devree2021,ogum2024}. DEP-RL uses the same direct
muscle-level action space, but adds DEP-style embodied exploration
\cite{deprl2023}. The proposed Residual-Reflex RL controller learns
four-dimensional residual actions that modulate the selected reflex parameters
in Table~\ref{tab:residual_actions}. The CMA-ES-optimized reflex
controller is the reflex controller obtained by offline parameter optimization;
its parameters are not updated during evaluation. It is used as a non-learning
reflex baseline in the weakness and perturbation tests. All DRL policies were
trained with MPO-based off-policy reinforcement learning on the same
musculoskeletal model.

\subsection{Nominal Walking Evaluation}
The nominal walking experiment evaluates whether the proposed controller
generates plausible steady gait under controlled target-speed tasks. Policies
were evaluated at target walking speeds of 0.8, 1.0, and 1.2~m/s. For each
trained policy, a 25s rollout was exported as an STO file for post-hoc gait
analysis. All detected gait cycles in the rollout were normalized to 0--100\%
gait cycle. The comparison
includes E2E-RL, DEP-RL, and Residual-Reflex RL. Four groups of metrics are
used, including task-level speed and stride measures, human-reference gait
profiles, GRF morphology, and symmetry/repeatability.

\subsubsection{Speed tracking and stride measures}
To verify that the policies satisfy the commanded locomotion task, we report
realized walking speed, absolute speed error
$|\hat{v}-v_{\mathrm{target}}|$, stride length, stride time, and the number of
analyzed cycles. Lower speed error indicates better task tracking, whereas
stride length and stride time describe the gait strategy used to achieve the
target speed. The stride measures were computed from the detected gait cycles
in each evaluated trajectory.

\subsubsection{Human-reference kinematic gait analysis}
To quantify whether the generated joint motion resembles human walking, we
compared the simulated gait-cycle profiles with human-reference gait data for
hip angle, knee angle, and ankle angle. The gray bands in
the profile figures denote lower and upper bounds derived from the reference
data. They were used only for post-hoc evaluation and were not included in the
policy reward.
For each quantity, left and right gait cycles were time-normalized
and averaged to obtain a mean simulated profile $\bar{y}(x)$, where $x$
denotes gait-cycle percentage. Let $y_{\min}(x_k)$ and $y_{\max}(x_k)$ denote
the lower and upper human-reference bounds at sample point $x_k$. The
normalized reference-range violation is
\begin{equation}
e = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
\frac{
\left[y_{\min}(x_k)-\bar{y}(x_k)\right]_+
+ \left[\bar{y}(x_k)-y_{\max}(x_k)\right]_+
}{
\max\left(0.01, y_{\max}(x_k)-y_{\min}(x_k)\right)
},
\end{equation}
where $[z]_+=\max(z,0)$.
The corresponding score is
\begin{equation}
S = 100 \cdot \mathrm{clip}(1-e,0,1).
\end{equation}
Higher scores indicate that the averaged trajectory remains closer to the
human-reference range; a score of 100 means that the mean profile stays entirely
inside the reference bounds. The kinematic average score is the mean of the hip,
knee, and ankle component scores.

\subsubsection{GRF morphology}
Because vertical GRF directly reflects stance loading and push-off behavior, we
also visualize individual left and right GRF gait cycles. This analysis is used
to assess whether the controller produces repeatable stance-phase loading,
reasonable double-support/propulsion morphology, and avoids abnormal impact
peaks. The numerical GRF score is the GRF component of the human-reference
range metric, applied separately to vertical GRF, while the cycle plots show
left--right and stride-to-stride consistency.

\subsubsection{Symmetry and repeatability}
Finally, bilateral symmetry was quantified as the root-mean-square difference
between the average left and right normalized profiles. For a gait-cycle
quantity $y$, this waveform difference is
\begin{equation}
D_y =
\sqrt{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}
\left(\bar{y}_{L}(x_k)-\bar{y}_{R}(x_k)\right)^2},
\end{equation}
where $y$ denotes a normalized gait-cycle waveform, $x_k$ is the $k$th
gait-cycle sample, $N$ is the number of samples in the normalized gait cycle,
and $\bar{y}_{L}$ and $\bar{y}_{R}$ are the average left- and right-side
profiles computed over the analyzed cycles. In the results, lower $D_y$
indicates more symmetric left--right motion.

Stride-to-stride repeatability was quantified using the coefficient of
variation (CV) of stride time and stride length.
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CV}_{z}=100\frac{\sigma_z}{\mu_z},
\end{equation}
where $z$ denotes either stride time or stride length, $\mu_z$ is the mean
value of $z$, and $\sigma_z$ is its standard deviation over the analyzed gait
cycles. The factor 100 expresses CV as a percentage. In the results, lower
$\mathrm{CV}_z$ indicates more repeatable gait cycles.
Accordingly, the Hip, Knee, Ankle, and GRF columns in the symmetry table report
$D_y$ for the corresponding left--right waveform pair, whereas the Time CV and
Length CV columns report $\mathrm{CV}_z$ for stride time and stride length.

\subsection{Plantarflexor Weakness Adaptation}
The weakness experiment evaluates whether the trained residual-reflex controller
can remain usable after a change in muscle capacity without retraining. This
directly tests the motivation for using a state-dependent policy on top of the
reflex controller, rather than relying only on one offline-optimized parameter
set. Plantarflexor weakness was simulated by reducing the maximum isometric
force of the soleus muscles while keeping the trained 1.2~m/s policy fixed. The
evaluated soleus strength levels were 83--100\% of the nominal value.
Residual-Reflex RL was compared with the CMA-ES-optimized reflex controller
under the same weakened models. We report survival length, realized speed, gait
quality, and the root-mean-square magnitude of the four residual actions; for
the CMA-ES-optimized reflex controller, we report rollout duration as the
failure/survival measure.
\begin{equation}
A_i^{\mathrm{RMS}} =
\sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}a_i(t)^2},
\end{equation}
where $a_i(t)$ is the $i$th residual action and $T$ is the number of evaluated
policy steps in the rollout. The RMS value measures how strongly the policy
uses each reflex-parameter channel regardless of sign. This is important because
a residual action can increase or decrease a reflex parameter at different
moments of the gait cycle.

\subsection{External Push Perturbation}
The perturbation experiment evaluates whether the trained residual-reflex
controller can retain closed-loop walking after an unexpected environmental
disturbance, again without retraining. The same trained 1.2~m/s policy used in
the nominal walking test was kept unchanged during perturbation evaluation.
Backward force pulses were applied to the torso starting at 3s. We tested 75
and 100N pushes, including both single pulses and repeated pulses every 5s.
Residual-Reflex RL was compared with the CMA-ES-optimized reflex controller
under the same perturbation settings. We report rollout duration and realized
speed after evaluation without further learning. These metrics distinguish
sustained recovery from a brief delayed fall after the push.

\section{Results and Analysis}
\label{sec:results-and-analysis}
The results are organized to match the three evaluation blocks in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}. We first analyze nominal walking at the three
target speeds, then examine plantarflexor weakness adaptation, and finally
evaluate external push perturbation recovery.

\subsection{Nominal Walking Evaluation}

the controlled speed tasks. Table~\ref{tab:spatiotemporal_metrics} shows that Residual-Reflex RL tracked the commanded
speed most closely at all three target speeds, with absolute speed errors of
0.01, 0.05, and 0.01~m/s. E2E-RL and DEP-RL also produced

conditions. For example, E2E-RL overshot the 1.0~m/s target, whereas DEP-RL

shorter and faster steps at lower speed, while DEP-RL and Residual-Reflex RL use
longer strides. These task-level measures therefore motivate the following
gait-cycle analyses, which examine whether successful walking is also
accompanied by human-reference joint motion, contact loading, and repeatable

\subsubsection{GRF morphology}
Ground reaction force provides a direct view of how each controller loads and
unloads the body during stance. Residual-Reflex RL produced smoother vertical
GRF profiles with a more reference-consistent double-support pattern across the
tested speeds, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:grf_scores} and illustrated in
Fig.~\ref{fig:grf_morphology}. E2E-RL showed
abnormal or inconsistent loading despite visually plausible joint postures in
some snapshots; this explains why its GRF scores were much lower than its hip
or ankle scores. DEP-RL produced even larger early-stance peaks and
stride-to-stride fluctuations at 1.0 and 1.2~m/s, leading to near-zero GRF
agreement in those cases. In the GRF metric, a score of 0.0 means that the mean
force profile stays outside the human-reference range after normalization, which
matches the large impact-like peaks and irregular unloading visible in the DEP-RL
panels. Residual-Reflex RL avoided these sharp loading transients and achieved
the highest GRF scores at all three speeds.
This is an important failure mode observed in E2E-RL and DEP-RL because the limbs can
appear visually plausible in some frames, but still interact with the ground
through abrupt or poorly timed contact forces. The smoother GRF curves of
Residual-Reflex RL suggest that the reflex prior helps organize the stance
phase, not only the joint-angle trajectories.

The residual reflex representation also improved the regularity of the generated
gait cycles. Table~\ref{tab:symmetry_variability} shows that Residual-Reflex RL
consistently produced lower left--right waveform differences and lower stride

average left and right gait-cycle waveforms for each quantity. The Time CV and

length, computed as standard deviation divided by mean and multiplied by 100.
The largest differences occurred in ankle symmetry and stride variability, where
E2E-RL and DEP-RL produced visibly less repeatable cycles. These metrics support
the visual observation that residual reflex modulation yields a more bilaterally
symmetric and repeatable gait pattern, rather than merely producing occasional
successful walking cycles.

Residual-Reflex RL were 0.70\% and 0.74\%, respectively, both below 1\%.

that the standard deviation of stride timing and length was less than 1\% of
their corresponding mean values. E2E-RL and DEP-RL showed larger
cycle-to-cycle variation. Thus, the proposed method did not only produce a good
average gait cycle; it also generated more consistent steps throughout the
analyzed trajectory.

\subsection{Plantarflexor Weakness Adaptation}
\label{sec:weakness_results}
The weakness experiment examines whether the learned reflex-parameter
modulation remains usable when the musculoskeletal model changes after
training. The soleus maximum isometric force was reduced while the trained
1.2~m/s Residual-Reflex RL policy was kept unchanged, so the controller was
evaluated on weakened models without additional training or parameter
optimization. Under the same model changes, the CMA-ES-optimized reflex
controller terminated early for all tested weakened models, whereas
Residual-Reflex RL produced full 1000-step rollouts for soleus strength levels
from 83\% to 100\%, as summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:weakness_survival}.

Table~\ref{tab:weakness_adaptation} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:weakness_speed_adaptation}
show that the residual policy did not simply force the weakened model to keep
the nominal 1.2~m/s speed. Instead, the realized speed decreased as soleus
strength was reduced, from 1.20~m/s in the nominal model to 0.95~m/s at 83\%
soleus strength. This behavior is consistent with a conservative adaptation
strategy and resembles a common compensatory response in human gait. When lower
limb force capacity is reduced, people often reduce walking speed to maintain
comfort, stability, and coordinated limb motion. Similarly, the controller
preserves stable and natural-looking walking by accepting a slower gait rather
than maintaining the target speed at the cost of instability.
Importantly, the slower gait was not simply a degraded survival behavior. The
generated joint trajectories remained close to the human-reference kinematic
profiles across the tested weakness levels, as shown in
Table~\ref{tab:weakness_kinematic_scores} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:weakness_kinematic_profiles}. Even at 83\% soleus strength, the
policy still produced a full rollout with a kinematic-average score of
89.0\%, indicating that the changed muscle model could be accommodated without
retraining while preserving a natural gait pattern.

The residual-action RMS values also show that the policy remains actively
engaged under the weakened conditions. The four channels correspond directly to
the selected reflex pathways in Table~\ref{tab:residual_actions}. Specifically, $a_1$
modulates hamstrings length feedback for hip extension and late-swing braking,
$a_2$ modulates iliopsoas length-offset feedback for hip flexion and swing
initiation, $a_3$ modulates soleus force feedback for ankle support and
propulsion, and $a_4$ modulates vasti force feedback for knee extension and
stance support. The soleus-related channel $a_3$ has the largest RMS magnitude
across the weakness range, indicating that ankle support/propulsion remains the
dominant modulated pathway even when the soleus force capacity is altered.
However, the policy does not simply increase the soleus-related action as the
muscle becomes weaker. Instead, it accepts a lower walking speed and
redistributes modulation across the other pathways. In particular, the
vasti-related channel $a_4$ is more strongly engaged under larger weakness,
which is consistent with increased reliance on knee-extension support when
ankle push-off capacity is reduced. The hamstrings channel $a_1$ and iliopsoas
channel $a_2$ vary more mildly, helping adjust late-swing braking, swing
initiation, leg placement, and step timing. The paired trends between the hip
extension/flexion channels ($a_1$, $a_2$) and the ankle/knee support channels
($a_3$, $a_4$) suggest a coordinated redistribution of reflex modulation rather
than a single-parameter compensation. These results indicate that the
four-dimensional residual action space acts as a compact adaptive interface on
top of the reflex controller. The same trained policy can be transferred across
weakened body models without retraining, while still modulating the underlying
reflex pathways online.

\subsection{External Push Perturbation}
\label{sec:perturbation_results}
The external push experiment further evaluates whether the learned residual
policy can tolerate environmental disturbances that were not part of training.
The trained 1.2~m/s Residual-Reflex RL policy was evaluated directly, with no
additional learning or parameter tuning. Backward force pulses were applied to
the torso at 3s. The CMA-ES-optimized reflex controller terminated after
approximately 4.5--4.6s for both the single and repeated 75/100N settings. In
contrast, Residual-Reflex RL produced representative full-episode recoveries
under the same 75/100N single-push conditions.

Table~\ref{tab:push_perturbation} summarizes the perturbation outcomes. Under
single backward pushes of 75 and 100N, Residual-Reflex RL maintained walking
for the full 25s evaluation window. Repeated perturbations every 5s were
substantially harder. The 75N repeated-push case still produced a full rollout,
whereas the 100N case lasted 20.1s. In comparison, the CMA-ES-optimized reflex
controller did not recover beyond roughly 4.5s in the same perturbation
settings. This pattern indicates that the learned reflex-parameter policy
provides additional recovery capacity beyond the fixed optimized reflex gains.
Together with the weakness test, these results support the central claim that
the learned residual policy provides a flexible reflex-parameter modulation
mechanism beyond offline optimized reflex gains.

\subsection{Discussion}
The results indicate that the main benefit of residual reflex learning is a
more structured and physiologically plausible gait pattern together with a
limited but measurable form of adaptability. In the nominal-speed data, E2E-RL
and DEP-RL can achieve successful forward walking, yet their gait-cycle profiles
may deviate substantially from reference kinematics and GRF. This observation
suggests that learned musculoskeletal gait should be assessed not only by task
completion, but also through gait-cycle kinematics, GRF morphology, bilateral
symmetry, and cycle-to-cycle repeatability.

The observed improvement is consistent with the role of the reflex prior. In
E2E-RL and DEP-RL, the policy must discover both the low-level muscle
coordination and the task-level walking strategy directly in the muscle action
space. In Residual-Reflex RL, the baseline reflex controller already provides
phase-dependent sensory feedback and stance support, while the learned policy
adjusts only a small set of interpretable reflex parameters. This restriction
does not merely reduce dimensionality; it biases exploration toward coordinated
changes in existing neuromechanical feedback pathways. The more clustered
kinematic cycles, smoother GRF profiles, and lower stride variability are
consistent with this structured action representation. The weakness and
perturbation results further clarify why learning on top of the reflex
controller is useful even when an optimized reflex baseline can walk in the
nominal condition. A CMA-ES-optimized reflex controller uses feedback, but its
parameters are selected offline and then held constant during evaluation.
Residual-Reflex RL keeps the same feedback pathways while allowing the policy to
reshape their effective gains and offsets online. This distinction becomes visible when muscle
capacity is reduced or an external push is applied, because the residual policy can
trade off speed, posture, and reflex modulation to maintain walking, whereas the
offline optimized reflex parameters are more brittle.


\section{Conclusion}
We presented a Residual-Reflex RL framework for musculoskeletal gait
generation. Rather than directly commanding all muscle
excitations, the learned policy modulates four gait-relevant reflex parameters
around a reflex-based controller. This formulation reduces the effective action
space and preserves a structured neuromechanical prior within the control loop.

Experiments in a Hyfydy-based musculoskeletal simulation showed that the
Residual-Reflex RL controller produced gait cycles with higher
human-reference gait scores than E2E-RL and DEP-RL at 0.8, 1.0, and 1.2~m/s.
The improvement was most evident in knee kinematics, ankle kinematics, and
vertical GRF morphology. Additional cycle-level analysis showed lower
left--right waveform differences and lower stride variability, indicating more
regular and symmetric gait patterns. Beyond nominal walking, the same trained
policy preserved full-episode walking under reduced soleus strength by adopting
a slower gait and modulating the selected reflex parameters. It also recovered
from representative external push perturbations that caused the
CMA-ES-optimized reflex controller to terminate.
These findings suggest that reflex-parameter modulation can provide a compact
and interpretable learning interface for musculoskeletal gait synthesis, where
task success must be accompanied by physiologically plausible joint motion and
contact loading. Such a framework may be useful for simulation-based studies of
human locomotion, rehabilitation-oriented controller design, and assistive
device control where both adaptability and neuromuscular interpretability are
important. Future work will extend the framework to online speed transitions,
more systematic perturbation-recovery protocols, and broader musculoskeletal
models.
